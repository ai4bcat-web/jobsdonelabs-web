The hidden cost of untracked downtime

Here's a scenario that plays out in small manufacturing shops every day: Machine #3 goes down at 10:15 AM. The operator calls the shift supervisor, who's on the other side of the floor dealing with a quality issue. Supervisor gets to the machine at 10:45, diagnoses the problem for 30 minutes, calls maintenance at 11:15. Maintenance arrives at 1:30 PM because they were tied up on another job. The fix takes 90 minutes. Machine #3 is back up at 3:00 PM — 4 hours and 45 minutes after it stopped. Production lost: ~$712 at $150/hour.

Nobody wrote any of this down. Next week, Machine #3 goes down again — same root cause, same diagnostic loop, same lost production. The shop owner knows downtime happens but can't tell you how much, which machines, or why. When asked "what's your biggest downtime driver?" the answer is a guess. And guesses don't fix anything.

The downtime math most shops are ignoring:
A 10-machine shop averaging 2 hours of unplanned downtime per machine per month: 20 hours × $150/hr production value = $3,000/month. That's $36,000/year in direct production loss. Add indirect costs — overtime to catch up, expedited shipping on late orders, idle operators — and the real number is typically $50,000–72,000/year. The kicker: most shop owners estimate their downtime at 4–5 hours/month total across all machines. Systematic tracking reveals the actual number is routinely 3–6× higher.

The 3-part downtime tracking system (capture, categorize, analyze)

The system has three layers. Each builds on the previous one, and you can stop at any layer that meets your needs. Layer 1 takes 2 hours to set up and costs $0. Layer 3 adds OEE insight for shops that want it.

Layer 1: CAPTURE — log every stoppage as it happens

Every downtime event gets logged with 6 pieces of data:

  • Machine ID — which machine stopped (CNC-3, Lathe-2, Press-1)
  • Date — auto-populated in Google Forms
  • Downtime Start — when the machine stopped producing (operator enters time)
  • Downtime End — when it resumed producing
  • Duration — auto-calculated in the Sheet (=End minus Start)
  • Reason Code — dropdown: Mechanical, Electrical, Operator Error, Setup/Changeover, Material Wait, Quality Stop, Unknown

The data entry path: Google Form (loaded on a shop floor tablet or the operator's phone) feeds directly into a Google Sheet. No walking to an office terminal. No transcribing paper. Setup takes 60 minutes: create the form, add the 6 fields, share the link with operators.

Critical culture point: The form has no operator name field. This is deliberate. Downtime tracking fails when operators think it's a blame system. The goal is to identify system-level patterns ("Machine #3 has a recurring electrical fault"), not to punish individuals. Communicate this clearly before rollout.

Reason codes turn unstructured "machine was down" into actionable data. Seven codes cover 95% of small-shop downtime:

Layer 3: ANALYZE — the 15-minute weekly review that finds your fix

Every Friday, pull up the Google Sheet and create a pivot table with two dimensions:

  1. Downtime by Machine — which machines are down the most? You may discover that 2 out of 10 machines account for 70% of all downtime.
  2. Downtime by Reason Code — which categories dominate? If "Mechanical" is 60% of your downtime and 80% of that is on one machine, you know exactly where to look.

Build a simple Pareto chart (bar chart sorted by total hours) and focus on the top 2–3 bars. The principle: 80% of your downtime comes from 20% of the causes. Spending a week investigating your #1 downtime driver yields 10× the return of spreading effort across 10 small problems.

OEE for people who don't have time for OEE

Full OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness) requires tracking Availability × Performance × Quality across every shift — which is why most small shops never do it. But you can get 90% of the insight with 5 data points and a $0 Google Sheet:

OEE-Lite: 5 inputs

  1. Planned Production Time — shift length minus scheduled breaks and planned maintenance
  2. Actual Run Time — Planned Production Time minus unplanned downtime (from your tracking sheet)
  3. Ideal Cycle Time — the theoretical minimum time per part (from machine specs or best observed)
  4. Total Parts Produced — good parts + scrap, for the shift
  5. Good Parts — parts that passed inspection

The formulas auto-calculate in your Sheet:

  • Availability = Actual Run Time / Planned Production Time
  • Performance = (Total Parts × Ideal Cycle Time) / Actual Run Time
  • Quality = Good Parts / Total Parts
  • OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality

The real power isn't the OEE number itself — it's knowing which component is dragging it down. If Availability is 62% but Performance and Quality are both 95%+, your problem is machine uptime, not speed or scrap. Focus maintenance there. If Performance is 58% (machine running slow because of worn tooling), speed improvements are your highest ROI. OEE-Lite tells you where to point your effort.

How to use the data to actually fix things (the 5-week improvement cadence)

The most common failure mode: a shop tracks downtime for 2 weeks, sees data that feels overwhelming, and abandons the system. The fix is a structured cadence that starts slow and builds momentum:

The 5-week downtime reduction cadence

  1. Weeks 1–2: Track only, don't fix anything. Build the baseline. Operators log every stoppage. Supervisors don't intervene — they observe. The goal is clean data, not immediate improvements. You'll be surprised by what the baseline reveals.
  2. Week 3: Identify top 3 downtime drivers. Run the Pareto analysis. Which 2–3 reason codes on which 2–3 machines account for 70%+ of total downtime hours? Write them on a whiteboard in the shop.
  3. Week 4: Implement one countermeasure. Pick the #1 driver and implement ONE fix. Root-cause the specific pattern (5-Why analysis), implement the fix, and document it. Don't try to fix all three at once — you'll scatter focus and fix nothing.
  4. Week 5: Measure improvement. Compare this week's downtime for that machine/reason against the baseline. Did it drop? By how much? Annualize the recovery: (hours saved per week × $150/hr × 50 weeks). Celebrate the win, then pick the next driver.
  5. Repeat monthly. Every month, you pick the top downtime driver, fix the root cause, measure the improvement, and move to the next. Over 6 months, you'll have eliminated 5–8 systemic issues. This is how shops go from "we're always fighting fires" to "we know what to expect and it's getting better."

When to graduate from spreadsheet to CMMS

The Google Sheets system works for shops with fewer than 20 machines and a single shift. You know it's time to graduate to a CMMS (Computerized Maintenance Management System) when you hit these trigger points:

  • 20+ machines — the pivot tables get unwieldy
  • Multiple shifts — handoff communication between shifts becomes critical and Sheets don't handle shift-overlap analysis well
  • ISO/AS9100 requirement — auditors want timestamped maintenance records with revision control that Sheets can't easily provide
  • You're spending more time managing the Sheet than fixing machines — the tool should serve you, not the other way around

How a 12-machine CNC job shop found $52,000/year in hidden downtime and fixed the top 2 causes in 30 days

An Ohio CNC job shop with 12 machines had been running for 9 years. The owner estimated "maybe 4–5 hours of downtime a month across the whole shop." They started the Google Sheets tracking system and discovered the real number: 22 hours per week — nearly 6× the estimate. Two patterns dominated the data:

  1. Tooling breakage on one specific CNC lathe model (3 machines, accounting for 41% of downtime). Root cause: operators were running tool inserts past their rated life because "we don't have a replacement schedule." Fix: implemented a simple tool-life counter in the Sheet — after X parts, a flag pops up to change the insert during scheduled breaks, not during production. Cost: $0. Result: tooling-related downtime dropped from 9 hours/week to 1.5 hours/week.
  2. Material-wait because one aluminum extrusion supplier was chronically late (32% of downtime). Root cause: the supplier had no on-time delivery requirement in their contract and the shop had no backup. Fix: added a PO delivery window clause to the contract (with a 5% penalty for late deliveries) and qualified a secondary supplier for the 3 most-used extrusion profiles. Cost: supplier negotiation time (4 hours of the owner's time) and 2 hours to set up the backup supplier account. Result: material-wait downtime dropped from 7 hours/week to under 1 hour.

Bottom line: Total downtime dropped from 22 hours/week to 5 hours/week. At $150/hour production value, that's 17 hours/week × $150/hr × 50 weeks = $127,500/year in recovered production capacity. Total investment: 2 hours to set up the Sheet, 15 minutes/week to run the pivot table, and 4 hours of supplier negotiation. They didn't buy a CMMS, didn't hire a maintenance manager, and didn't buy new machines — they just started measuring what was already happening.

The counterintuitive truth about downtime

The shops with the worst downtime aren't the ones with the oldest machines — they're the ones that don't measure it at all. A 30-year-old Bridgeport with a well-executed PM schedule and systematic downtime tracking will outperform a brand-new 5-axis CNC that nobody's monitoring. The system is more important than the equipment.

PART 2: Preventative Maintenance Scheduling

Stop the Downtime Before It Happens

Part 1 gave you the system to track every stoppage. Now Part 2 gives you the system to prevent those stoppages from happening in the first place. Together, tracking plus prevention is the complete maintenance management resource — the proactive half that turns your downtime data into a maintenance schedule that catches every service before it is overdue.

The free PM scheduling system that catches every service before it is overdue

A 15-machine shop has 15 different PM schedules. CNC #1 needs an oil change every 500 hours. CNC #2 needs spindle bearing inspection every 2,000 hours. The compressor needs a belt check monthly. The forklift needs hydraulic service quarterly. The HVAC needs filter changes biannually. Plus annual DOT and compliance inspections on any shop vehicles. If the shop runs 8,000 production hours per year and a critical machine goes down for 5 days because an oil change was missed, the cost is staggering: at $120/hour burden rate plus $8,000 in rush parts plus $5,000 in overtime to catch up on late customer orders, that is $15,000 to $25,000 in preventable downtime cost from one missed PM on one machine.

The PM scheduling spreadsheet has four components that work together:

The asset registry — know what you are maintaining

Every machine goes into the registry with six fields:

  • Machine Name — the operator-facing label ("CNC Mill #3," "Compressor — Main")
  • Serial Number — from the manufacturer's data plate, for service calls and warranty claims
  • Department — where the machine lives (Machining, Assembly, Finishing)
  • Criticality — A (production stops if this machine is down — no backup), B (production slows but continues — alternate machine available), C (non-critical support equipment)
  • Replacement Cost — what it costs to replace this machine if it is destroyed by neglect. This number makes the PM ROI real.
  • Current Hour Meter — the running-hours reading at last check, for interval-based PMs

The criticality rating drives priority. An A-rated machine with an overdue PM gets immediate attention. A C-rated machine with an overdue PM goes on next week's list. The hour-meter field is updated monthly — operators read the meter during the last shift of the month and enter it in the Sheet.

The PM task library — every service, every machine, every interval

For each machine, define every PM task with five fields:

  • Task Description — specific and actionable ("Replace spindle oil and filter," not "Service spindle")
  • Interval Type — Hours (running time), Cycles (parts produced), or Calendar (days since last service)
  • Interval Value — the number (every 500 hours, every 90 days, every 10,000 cycles)
  • Last Service Date — when this task was last completed
  • Last Service Reading — the hour meter or cycle counter at the last service, for interval-based calculations

The spreadsheet auto-calculates four fields that tell you exactly where you stand:

  • Next Due Date — Last Service Date + Interval Value (in days). For hour-based intervals: ((Last Service Reading + Interval Value minus Current Hour Meter) divided by average daily hours) + Today.
  • Due Status — OK (more than 14 days out), Upcoming (due within 14 days), or Overdue (past the due date). Color-coded green, yellow, red so the dashboard is scannable in 10 seconds.
  • Days Overdue — negative means upcoming, positive means overdue. Zero means due today.
  • Risk Level — combines Criticality and Due Status. A-Criticality + Overdue = Critical Risk (needs immediate attention). C-Criticality + Upcoming = Low Risk (schedule for next week).

PM scheduling dashboard — what you check every Monday morning

  1. Overdue PMs — sorted by risk level. Any A-Criticality machine with an overdue PM gets a maintenance tech assigned today.
  2. Upcoming PMs This Week — tasks due in the next 7 days. Assign them to specific techs and specific shifts so they do not get lost in the daily chaos.
  3. PM Compliance Rate — this month: completed PMs divided by scheduled PMs, as a percentage. Target: 90%+. Track this monthly and share it at the all-hands meeting.

The cost-of-deferral calculator — what skipping a PM actually costs

Every PM task has a deferral cost, and the calculator makes it visible. The logic is straightforward: skipping a PM accelerates wear on a component. The component fails earlier than its expected life. The replacement cost plus the production downtime cost is the deferral cost. The formula:

Deferral Cost = (Replacement Parts + Labor + Production Downtime Cost) x (Deferred Days / Expected Life Remaining)

Worked example — CNC spindle oil change:
PM cost: $200 (oil, filter, 1 hour of tech time).
Replacement cost if spindle seizes: $18,000 (new spindle + labor + realignment).
Production downtime: 4 days at $150/hour, 8 hours/day = $4,800.
Total failure cost: $18,000 + $4,800 = $22,800.
Expected life with regular PMs: 15,000 hours (~7.5 years at 2,000 hours/year).
If PM is deferred by 500 hours (3 months), bearing wear accelerates and the spindle fails at 6,000 hours instead of 15,000.
Deferred Days equivalent: 500 hours / 8 hours per day = 62.5 days.
Expected Life Remaining at last PM: 15,000 hours / 8 hours per day = 1,875 days.
Deferral Cost = $22,800 x (62.5 / 1,875) = $760.
The $200 oil change deferred 3 months costs $760 in accelerated failure — a 3.8:1 penalty on the deferred PM. And that is the conservative estimate. In practice, accelerated wear is nonlinear — the 500-hour deferral may reduce spindle life by far more than 500 hours. The calculator uses conservative linear estimates so it never overstates the risk.

The cost-of-deferral number is what changes behavior. Telling a supervisor "do not skip PMs" is a rule. Showing them "skipping this $200 oil change will cost you $760 in accelerated spindle failure — and that is the best-case scenario" is a decision framework. The supervisor can still defer the PM if production pressure demands it — but now they know the price tag.

The shift from reactive to proactive — how the PM system connects to the downtime tracker

Part 1 tracks every downtime event: date, machine, duration, cause, cost. Part 2 schedules PMs to prevent those events. The two spreadsheets together create a feedback loop:

  1. Monthly review: Compare PM compliance rate (from Part 2) against unplanned downtime hours (from Part 1). A shop at 90% PM compliance typically has 60-70% less unplanned downtime than a shop at 50% compliance. The data proves the ROI of maintenance to anyone who asks — including the owner who approved the maintenance budget.
  2. Root cause feed: When Part 1 shows a spike in "Mechanical" downtime on Machine #4, check Part 2. Is Machine #4 behind on PMs? If yes, the root cause is a scheduling gap, not a machine defect — fix the PM system, not the machine. If Machine #4 is current on all PMs and still failing, the root cause is a design or operational issue — investigate the machine itself.
  3. PM interval optimization: If Machine #7 has zero unplanned downtime and its PMs are always clean (no issues found during service), the PM interval may be too frequent. Extend it from 500 hours to 750 hours and re-evaluate after 3 months. Conversely, if Machine #2 has recurring failures between PMs, the interval is too long — shorten it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate machine downtime cost?

The formula: (Number of machines) × (Unplanned downtime hours per month) × (Hourly production value). For a 10-machine shop at $150/hr with 2 hours/month per machine: 20 hrs × $150 = $3,000/month, or $36,000/year. Add indirect costs (overtime, expedited shipping, idle labor) and the real number is 1.5–2× higher. The first month of tracking typically reveals 3–6× more downtime than the owner estimated.

What's the simplest way to track production downtime?

Clipboard + Google Sheets with 5 columns: Machine ID, Date, Start, End, Reason Code. Operators log events, supervisor runs a 15-minute pivot table on Fridays. Costs $0, takes 2 hours to set up. The cultural shift (tracking as improvement, not blame) matters more than the technology.

Can I track OEE without software?

Yes. OEE-Lite uses 5 data points: Planned Production Time, Actual Run Time, Ideal Cycle Time, Total Parts, Good Parts. Auto-calculate Availability × Performance × Quality in a Google Sheet. The real insight isn't the OEE number — it's knowing which component (Availability, Performance, or Quality) is the bottleneck so you focus effort where it matters.

What's the difference between planned and unplanned downtime?

Planned downtime is scheduled (PMs, changeovers, breaks). Unplanned downtime is the enemy — breakdowns, tooling failures, material shortages. The goal: shift downtime from unplanned to planned, then shrink the planned portion. Most shops discover that much of their "planned" downtime is actually unplanned when they start tracking systematically.

How much does machine downtime really cost a small manufacturer?

For a 10-machine shop: $36,000/year in direct production loss at minimum. Real case: a 12-machine CNC shop discovered 22 hrs/week of unplanned downtime (6× estimated), fixed the top 2 causes in 30 days, and recovered $127,500/year in production capacity — without buying new equipment or software.

What is the difference between preventative maintenance and reactive maintenance?

Preventative maintenance (PM) is scheduled service on a time or usage interval — oil changes every 500 hours, belt inspections every 3 months, bearing replacements at 2,000 hours. Reactive maintenance is fixing something after it breaks. The cost difference is enormous: a $200 oil change done on schedule prevents a $15,000 spindle replacement when the bearings seize from contaminated oil. Reactive maintenance costs 3-9x more than preventative across the equipment lifecycle. The shops with the lowest downtime are not the ones with the newest machines — they are the ones with the best PM compliance. A shop at 90% PM compliance typically has 60-70% less unplanned downtime than a shop at 50% compliance.

How do I create a PM schedule for a small machine shop?

Start with an asset registry: list every machine with make, model, serial number, department, and replacement cost. Build a PM task library per machine — define interval type (hours, cycles, calendar), interval value, and task description for each service. Record last service date and current hour-meter reading. The spreadsheet auto-calculates next due date, due status, and risk level. A 15-machine shop can set up a complete PM schedule in a single afternoon using a free Google Sheets template. The critical discipline is checking the dashboard every Monday morning and assigning upcoming PMs to maintenance techs for the week.

What happens if I skip a scheduled PM on a CNC machine?

Skipping a PM is gambling with the most expensive asset in the shop. A skipped $200 oil change on a CNC spindle does not cause immediate failure — damage accumulates silently over 6-12 months as contaminated oil accelerates bearing wear. When the spindle seizes, repair cost is $12,000-25,000 (replacement spindle, labor, realignment) plus 3-7 days of lost production. That single skipped PM cost the shop $15,000-25,000 — a 75:1 to 125:1 ROI on doing the PM on time. The cost-of-deferral calculator quantifies the risk for every PM task so the decision to skip is not a guess.

How do I calculate the ROI of preventative maintenance?

PM ROI = (Cost of avoided failures) / (Cost of PM program). Track three numbers: PM compliance rate, unplanned downtime hours per month, and total maintenance cost (PM labor + repair labor + parts). A shop at 90% PM compliance typically sees 60-70% less unplanned downtime than a shop at 50% compliance. Example: a 12-machine shop at 50% compliance with 22 hours/week of downtime at $150/hour = $3,300/week. Improving to 90% compliance drops downtime to roughly 7 hours/week = $1,050/week. Weekly savings: $2,250. Annual savings: $112,500. PM program cost: roughly 4 hours/week of maintenance tech time at $30/hour = $120/week or $6,240/year. PM ROI: $112,500 / $6,240 = 18:1. The spreadsheet tracks all three numbers automatically.
