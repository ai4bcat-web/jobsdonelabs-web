The Eighteen Thousand Dollar Windshield Tax: Why Your Crews Go Home When They Could Be Earning

Here is a Tuesday you have lived: The framing crew finishes the Anderson job at 2 PM. The Johnson job is a fifteen-minute drive away. The foundation is poured, the lumber is on site, and the crew of four could frame the garage walls before 5 PM. But nobody tells them. The GC is on another site. The office manager is handling a supplier dispute. The foreman texts "all done" and the response is a thumbs-up emoji. The crew goes home. Tomorrow morning, instead of driving fifteen minutes from Anderson, they will drive forty five minutes from the shop. Thirty extra minutes times four guys is two hours of non-billable drive time at forty five dollars per hour. Ninety dollars. One day. One crew.

Across four crews, this happens roughly twice per week per crew. Eight occurrences per week. Ninety dollars per occurrence times eight times per week times fifty weeks: thirty six thousand dollars in windshield tax. And that is before you count the lost billable hours — the two hours that crew could have been framing instead of driving.

The windshield tax in one number:

A four-person crew driving forty five minutes to the shop instead of fifteen minutes to the next job wastes two hours of non-billable time. At forty five dollars per hour for four crew members, that is two hundred seventy dollars in a single afternoon. This happens because your whiteboard says "Anderson — finish by 4 PM" but the crew finished at 2 PM and nobody had a plan for what happens next.

The Four Tabs That Replace "Just Go Home" With "Go to Johnson"

This free Google Sheets crew coordination spreadsheet has four tabs, each solving one piece of the finish-early problem. Together, they turn the question "what is Crew 3 doing right now?" into a sixty-second check instead of a series of phone calls that nobody has time to make.

Tab 1: Crew Status Dashboard (Where Is Everyone Right Now?)

The first problem is visibility. You do not know which crews finished early, which ones are running behind, and which ones are waiting on an inspection. The status dashboard gives every crew one status, updated by text or a quick call to the office:

  • On site — on schedule: Crew is working, on track to finish at estimated time. No action needed.
  • On site — ahead of schedule: Crew will finish early. This is the flag that triggers the next-job router. Without this flag, nobody knows the crew has extra capacity until they text "we are heading home."
  • On site — behind schedule: Crew is delayed. The next job they were supposed to start tomorrow may need to be re-assigned. Flag this early so the office can adjust.
  • Waiting on inspection: Crew cannot proceed until an inspector arrives. This is dead time that could be re-routed elsewhere. Flag the expected wait time.
  • Waiting on materials: Material delivery is late. Same as inspection — dead time until the materials arrive. If the wait is more than sixty minutes, route the crew elsewhere.
  • Finished — available for next job: Crew is done and ready for the next assignment. This is the status that triggers the router. If no next job is available, crew returns to shop or goes home — but at least that decision is made intentionally, not by default.

The dashboard uses color coding: green for on-schedule or finished-and-routed, yellow for ahead-of-schedule or behind-schedule (watch), and red for any status that means dead time (waiting on inspection, waiting on materials, or finished with no next job). One glance at the red cells and you know where money is burning.

Tab 2: Nearest-Next-Job Router (Who Goes Where Next?)

When a crew flips to "finished — available," the router finds the best next assignment. It does not replace your production schedule — it fills the gaps your schedule did not expect. Here is how it works:

  1. Enter open jobs that can be started now (foundation is poured, materials on site, no predecessor task blocking).
  2. Enter each job's zip code and required trade skills (framing, electrical, finish carpentry, etc.).
  3. Enter each crew's current zip code (where they finished the last job) and trade skills.
  4. The router scores each crew-to-job pair on three criteria: travel distance from current location (fifteen minutes beats forty five), trade-skill match (a framing crew should not be routed to an electrical rough-in), and job priority (the Johnson job that has lumber on site and a truss delivery Thursday beats the Smith job that has not had the foundation inspected yet).
  5. The top-scored match is the recommended next assignment. A dispatcher or GC makes the final call, but the spreadsheet does the sixty seconds of math that turns "nobody knows" into "send Crew 3 to Johnson."

Tab 3: Crew-to-Crew Handoff Log (What Does the Next Crew Need to Know?)

When one crew finishes and another crew picks up the same job tomorrow, critical information dissolves in the gap. A framing crew knows that master bedroom wall number three is one quarter inch out of square and needs shimming — but they went home at 3 PM and the finish carpentry crew arriving at 7 AM has no idea. They shim the drywall, the baseboard does not sit flush, and the homeowner calls about a gap. Forty five minutes of rework because one sentence was never conveyed.

The handoff log is a one-row-per-job running log that any crew can read before starting work:

  • Job: Which job site this note applies to.
  • From crew: Which crew left the note.
  • What the next crew needs to know: The specific issue. "Master bedroom wall #3 is 1/4 inch out of square — shim before drywall." Not "walls look good."
  • Photo reference: A cell-phone photo filename or link showing the issue. "See image IMG_4821 — north wall, laser level shows gap."
  • Priority: Must-read-before-starting, good-to-know, or FYI.
  • Resolved: Checkbox that the next crew marks when they have addressed the issue.

This replaces the text-message grapevine where a foreman sends a blurry photo to the GC who forwards it to the next foreman with the caption "this wall???". One log. One row per issue. No lost information.

Tab 4: Crew Utilization Tracker (Are Your Crews Earning or Driving?)

The utilization tracker answers the question you cannot answer from timesheets alone: what percentage of paid hours were actually billable? A crew that clocks eight hours but spent two hours driving between sites has only seventy five percent utilization. At forty five dollars per hour times four crew members, two hours of non-billable time costs three hundred sixty dollars. That is not overtime or material waste — it is pure routing waste.

How to Deploy This in One Week (Without Buying Software)

The 5-day launch plan

  1. Day 1 — Monday: Copy the spreadsheet template to your Google Drive. Enter all active crews (name, trade skills, current job, expected finish). Enter all open jobs with zip codes and required trades. This takes forty five minutes.
  2. Day 2 — Tuesday: Brief your foremen. The new expectation: when a crew is going to finish early, text the office. The office updates the status dashboard. The crew gets their next assignment before they pack up. No extra paperwork for the crew — they just text instead of deciding to go home.
  3. Day 3 — Wednesday: Run the router for the first time. When a crew texts "finishing early," pull up the router, find the best match, and send them. Even if the match is not perfect, you are building the muscle. The crew sees that finishing early means earning more, not going home early.
  4. Day 4 — Thursday: Start the handoff log. Ask each foreman for one thing the next crew needs to know about the job they are leaving. Write it in the log. Tell the arriving crew where to find it.
  5. Day 5 — Friday: Fill in the utilization tracker for the week. Look at the numbers. Which crew had the most drive time? Why? Is it a routing problem, a crew problem, or a schedule problem? Decide one change for next week.

By Friday, you have five days of real data on crew status, routing decisions, handoff notes, and utilization. You will know more about your crews' actual working time than you learned in the last six months of timesheet review. And you built it in a spreadsheet, not a twelve thousand dollar construction management platform.

Frequently asked questions

What is a crew coordination spreadsheet and why do I need one?

A crew coordination spreadsheet tracks where every crew is right now — on-site, finished, delayed, or waiting on inspection — identifies the nearest next job when a crew finishes early, and routes them there before they drive back to the shop. Without one, crews finishing early go home and drive from the shop the next day instead of from the current job site. This windshield tax — extra drive time from poor routing — costs a typical four-person crew roughly ninety dollars per day in wasted labor. Over two hundred working days, that is eighteen thousand dollars. The spreadsheet pays for itself in one week of avoided windshield time. It does not replace your production schedule or your project management software — it fills the real-time gap between "the schedule says 4 PM" and "the crew actually finished at 2 PM."

How is this different from a regular construction schedule or whiteboard?

A whiteboard or Gantt chart tells you where crews are supposed to be. This spreadsheet tells you where they are right now and where they should go next when plans change. The difference is real-time status versus a static plan. Plans change constantly — inspections run long, materials are late, a crew finishes framing two hours ahead of schedule. A static schedule does not adapt; a whiteboard in the office does not help the foreman at the job site. The crew coordination spreadsheet gives the foreman one thing to do — text "ahead of schedule" — and the office one thing to do — route them to the next job. Two people, two actions, sixty seconds. No software license required.

How much money does better crew routing actually save?

For a general contractor running four crews, the savings break into two categories. First: avoided windshield tax. When a crew finishing at 2 PM at Site A could start Site B fifteen minutes away but instead drives forty five minutes to the shop and then forty five minutes to Site B the next morning, you pay four crew members for ninety minutes of non-billable drive time instead of fifteen minutes. At forty five dollars per hour, that is two hundred seventy dollars in one afternoon. Second: recovered billable hours. Routing a finished crew to the next job turns non-billable windshield time into billable work. Across four crews doing this eight times per month, that is roughly six thousand dollars per month in recovered labor — seventy two thousand dollars per year. The spreadsheet pays for itself in under a week. The harder number to measure is schedule compression: when Johnson's garage walls get done Tuesday instead of Wednesday, the entire project timeline pulls forward one day. One day saved across four projects per year means your crews complete roughly one extra project annually without adding headcount.

What if my crews do not have smartphones or cell service on site?

Two backups cover this. First: the foreman calls the office at a scheduled check-in time — 11 AM and 3 PM. The status dashboard gets updated during those two windows, not in real time. You lose some responsiveness but still capture finish-early events within a few hours. Second: the foreman notes the handoff issue on the paper daily report (see our free construction daily report system) and the office transcribes it into the handoff log at end of day. The coordination still happens — just on a two-hour cycle instead of instantly. The spreadsheet is the record; it does not require real-time connectivity to be useful. Most crews do have phones, and a text takes ten seconds. The barrier is usually habit, not technology.

Does this spreadsheet replace construction project management software like Procore or Buildertrend?

No, and it is not trying to. Procore and Buildertrend handle the project — RFIs, submittals, drawings, budget. This spreadsheet handles the gap between the project plan and the daily reality of where your crews actually are and where they should go next. It is a coordination layer, not a project management layer. Most GCs running two to six million dollars in revenue have a project schedule (even if it is a whiteboard) but zero real-time crew coordination. The spreadsheet fills that gap for zero dollars. If you already have Procore and your crews are still driving forty five minutes past open jobs, the problem is not the software you own — it is that nobody pulls the real-time status into a routing decision. This spreadsheet lives alongside your project software, not instead of it.
