The Eighty Thousand Dollar Subcontractor Blind Spot

A general contractor at two million dollars in annual revenue typically spends approximately eight hundred thousand dollars per year on subcontractors across electrical, plumbing, drywall, roofing, concrete, and finishing trades. If ten percent of that spend goes to underperforming subcontractors through schedule delays, rework, and punch-list churn, that is eighty thousand dollars a year in preventable cost.

And yet most small to mid-size general contractors make the rehire decision based on memory and the last conversation. The electrical sub who was two weeks late on the school renovation nine months ago? You forgot. The drywall sub whose punch-list ran to forty seven items on the medical office build? You hired them again because they picked up the phone on the first ring and their bid was four thousand dollars lower.

Without a scorecard, every rehire decision is a bet. With a scorecard, it is math.

The real pain: "My company wants all the project managers to fill out a paper review form for our subcontractors every quarter so they can keep track of performance for future work." That is from an active thread on r/ConstructionManagers. Nine detailed answers from GCs who are all trying to solve the same problem with paper forms and quarterly meetings. One commenter summed it up: "We use the same three electrical subs but one is always two days late, another has three times the punch-list items, and I do not actually know if the third one is any good because I have never measured."

The Free Subcontractor Performance Scorecard Spreadsheet

The scorecard spreadsheet has four tabs, each built for a specific phase of the subcontractor management lifecycle. You do not need procurement software, a construction management platform, or a dedicated vendor management person. You need a Google Sheet and thirty minutes per completed project to fill it in.

Tab 1 — Per-Project Per-Sub Evaluation

For every subcontractor on every project, capture ten data points at project close:

  • Trade — electrical, plumbing, drywall, roofing, concrete, framing, HVAC, flooring, painting, finishing
  • Contract amount — the awarded bid value
  • Final amount — actual total including change orders
  • Budget variance percentage — how far the final amount deviated from the contract
  • Schedule variance in days — actual completion date minus scheduled completion date
  • Inspection pass rate — number of inspections passed on first attempt divided by total inspections
  • Punch-list count per ten thousand dollars of contract — a normalized metric so you can compare a fifty thousand dollar electrical sub to a twelve thousand dollar painting sub
  • Communication rating, one to five — did they return calls, flag issues early, and coordinate with other trades
  • Safety incidents — any recordable incident on this project
  • Would hire again, yes or no — the gut-check that matters most

Each sub-project combination gets one row. After five projects with the same electrical sub across eighteen months, you have five rows of quantitative performance data where you previously had a vague feeling.

Tab 2 — Aggregate Performance Dashboard

The dashboard rolls up every row from Tab 1 into a single view ranked by trade. For each subcontractor you see:

  • Composite score — a weighted average of the five core metrics. Budget variance and schedule variance carry the highest weight because they hit the bottom line directly. Communication and inspection pass rate carry moderate weight because they predict future performance. Punch-list count carries lower weight because it varies significantly by trade and project complexity.
  • Project count — how many projects this sub has completed for you, which matters because a four-point-eight score across seven projects is more reliable than a four-point-eight score across one project
  • Twelve-month trend line — is the score improving, stable, or declining
  • Total estimated delay and rework cost — the spreadsheet calculates this from schedule variance multiplied by your daily general conditions cost plus punch-list items multiplied by your average punch-list resolution cost
  • Rank within trade — first, second, third among all electrical subs, all plumbing subs, and so on

The bid-leveling calculator is the single feature that pays for the entire spreadsheet on the first project you use it for. When an owner or project stakeholder pushes back on why you did not take the lowest bid, you have a one-page analysis showing exactly why the low bid was not the low cost.

Tab 4 — Trend Detection Dashboard

A subcontractor who was four-point-eight for three years and drops to three-point-nine over the last six months is not having a bad quarter. They are in decline, and the decline will accelerate. The trend detection dashboard flags this pattern automatically:

  • Declining score flag — any sub whose twelve-month trend line shows a drop of more than one full point triggers a yellow alert. More than two full points triggers a red alert.
  • Rising punch-list flag — any sub whose punch-list count per ten thousand dollars has doubled over the last three projects triggers a yellow alert. This is the earliest warning sign of a declining sub, often appearing six to twelve months before schedule or budget problems surface.
  • Cold sub flag — any sub you have not hired in twelve months who previously had strong scores. Maybe they are still good and you forgot about them. Maybe their best foreman left and the replacement is not as sharp. The flag prompts a check-in call before you write them off.

The trend dashboard catches the decline at project three when a conversation can still fix it. Without it, you discover the problem at project six when the sub fails on a large scope and you are explaining to the owner why the project is three weeks behind schedule.

The First Thirty Minutes — What to Do Right Now

You do not need to go back and score every sub on every project from the last five years. Start with the three projects that are currently active or just completed:

Setup checklist

  1. Copy the spreadsheet template into your Google Drive. Rename it "Sub Scorecard — [Your Company Name]."
  2. List your active subs. On Tab 1, add one row for every subcontractor currently on an active project. You should have roughly ten to twenty rows for a typical mid-size GC.
  3. Score the last completed project for each. Fill in the ten data points from memory or your project closeout notes. Partial data is better than no data. If you do not know the exact punch-list count, estimate it and mark the row with a "needs verification" note.
  4. Check the dashboard. Tab 2 will auto-populate. You will see your first leaderboard immediately. One sub will already stand out as your best. One will already stand out as your worst. You probably already knew which ones they were, but now you have numbers backing it up.
  5. Schedule the first quarterly review. Put a thirty-minute recurring meeting on your calendar for the first Friday after each quarter closes. Open Tab 2, review the leaderboard, identify any declining subs, and decide who gets the next call and who does not.

Total time: less than one hour. After that, each new project adds roughly fifteen minutes of data entry at closeout. For a GC running six to ten projects per year, that is roughly two to three hours of annual maintenance in exchange for eighty thousand dollars in underperformance cost avoidance.

Frequently asked questions

How do I evaluate subcontractor performance objectively?

Use the five weighted metrics from Tab 1: budget variance, schedule variance, inspection pass rate, punch-list count per ten thousand dollars of contract, and communication rating. Score every sub on every project. After five projects with the same sub across eighteen months, you have twenty five data points where you previously had a gut feeling. The aggregate dashboard removes recency bias: the sub who was great last month but terrible for the previous six months gets an honest average, not a pass based on their most recent project.

What metrics should a subcontractor scorecard track?

Track ten metrics per sub per project: trade, contract amount, final amount, budget variance percentage, schedule variance in days, inspection pass rate, punch-list count per ten thousand dollars, communication rating on a one-to-five scale, safety incidents, and a would-hire-again flag. Seven of these ten are quantitative. Three (communication, safety, would-hire-again) capture the things that spreadsheets often miss. Together they give you a complete picture that neither numbers alone nor gut feel alone can provide.

How do I compare subcontractor bids beyond just the price?

The bid-leveling calculator in Tab 3 adds each bidder's historical delay cost and rework cost to their bid amount to produce a true cost per bidder. A low bid of thirty eight thousand dollars from a sub with a three-point-one score and a history of two thousand one hundred dollars in average delay cost plus one thousand eight hundred dollars in average rework cost has a true cost of approximately forty one thousand nine hundred dollars. Compare that to a forty two thousand dollar bid from a four-point-six sub with no history of delays or rework. The spreadsheet makes the argument quantitatively: the higher bid is the better value.

How often should I update subcontractor performance scores?

Three checkpoints per project: fifty percent completion for a mid-project pulse check, within one week of substantial completion for the final evaluation, and at the thirty-day warranty walkthrough for any post-completion issues. The mid-project check is the highest-leverage update: a sub who was excellent for three years but whose last six months show slipping scores needs a conversation at project three, not after project six fails.

What is the difference between a subcontractor scorecard and subcontractor paperwork management?

A subcontractor paperwork management system tracks documents: COIs, W-9s, contracts, and expiration dates. Our free paperwork management system handles the compliance side. A subcontractor scorecard tracks performance: quality, schedule, budget, and communication. The paperwork system tells you whether a sub is legally cleared to work. The scorecard tells you whether you want them to work. A GC running multiple projects needs both.
