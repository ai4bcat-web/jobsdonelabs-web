The Pre-Appointment Reminder Sequence That Cuts No-Shows by 25%

Most practices send one reminder 24 hours before the appointment. That catches the forgetful patients — but it doesn't catch the ones who booked three weeks ago and forgot they even have an appointment, or the ones who double-booked and will decide at the last minute which to keep.

A 3-text + 1-email sequence before every appointment:

TimingChannelMessage Template
7 days out Text "Hi [Name], your appointment with Dr. [Provider] is next [Day] at [Time]. Reply C to confirm or R if you need to reschedule. — [Practice Name]"
3 days out Email "Just a quick reminder — Dr. [Provider] will see you this [Day] at [Time]. Need to change it? Use this link: [Booking Link]. Otherwise, we'll see you then."
24 hours out Text "See you tomorrow at [Time], [Name]! Our address is [Address]. Parking is [Directions]. Call [Phone] if anything changes."
2 hours out Text "Dr. [Provider] is ready for you at [Time]. Running late? Just reply to this text. — [Practice Name]"

Why four touches work better than one: The 7-day text catches the patients who booked weeks ago and genuinely forgot. The 3-day email gives them a window to reschedule before the 24-hour cancellation deadline — which means that slot can be refilled. The 24-hour text is the traditional reminder. And the 2-hour text catches the "I'm running 10 minutes late but I wasn't going to call" patients who would otherwise no-show out of embarrassment.

Cost to implement: $0 if you use your front desk phone for texts + your existing EHR's email reminder feature. Or $15-25/month for a Google Voice number dedicated to appointment reminders. The entire sequence can be automated with free tools — or you can build a custom n8n workflow that pulls tomorrow's appointments from your EHR at 6 PM and sends all four touches on schedule without a human touching anything.

The 3-Strike Reschedule Protocol (After They No-Show)

Most practices make one call, leave no voicemail, and the patient disappears. The 3-strike protocol changes the shape of the follow-up curve — strike 1 gets 20% back, strike 2 gets another 15%, and strike 3 catches the final 5%.

Strike 1 — Same Day (within 2 hours of missed appointment)

Text or call. "Hi [Name], we missed you at [Time] today. Everything okay? Would you like to reschedule? You can reply to this text or call us at [Phone] and we'll get you back in right away."

Tone check: concerned, not annoyed. You're checking on their wellbeing, not scolding them.

Strike 2 — 48 Hours Later

Text with a booking link. "Hi [Name], we still have you marked as missing your [Day] appointment with Dr. [Provider]. Want to grab a new slot? Here's a direct booking link: [Link]. If the timing doesn't work anymore, just let us know — no worries."

Why a booking link works: it removes the friction of calling the office, waiting on hold, and negotiating time slots. They can see availability and book in 30 seconds.

Strike 3 — 7 Days After

Mailed letter or final text. For established patients: "Hi [Name], we noticed you haven't rescheduled your [Month] appointment with Dr. [Provider]. We want to make sure you're taken care of — is there a reason the timing didn't work? Call us at [Phone] or reply here and we'll find something that fits."

For new patients (high-value — you spent $50-$150 to acquire them): skip the text. Mail a short handwritten note on practice letterhead. "We were looking forward to meeting you on [Date]. If the timing was inconvenient, we'd love to find a better slot — just call [Phone] and ask for [Name]. We saved a priority appointment for you within 48 hours." This is overkill for established patients but exactly right for new ones.

New-Patient No-Show Recovery: You Spent $150 to Acquire Them — Don't Let Them Disappear

New patients are the most expensive no-shows in your practice. A primary care practice spends $50-$150 in marketing to acquire each new patient (SEO, Google Ads, physician referrals, insurance directory listings). When that patient no-shows their first appointment and nobody follows up, you lit $150 on fire — and they'll book with a competitor next week.

The New-Patient Recovery Protocol

  1. Same-day phone call (not text). A new patient needs to hear a human being. "Hi [Name], this is [Staff Name] from [Practice]. We had you on the schedule for 10 AM and wanted to check in — is everything okay?" Do NOT sound annoyed. Sound like you're genuinely concerned they didn't make it.
  2. Offer the "priority reschedule." "We actually hold a couple of 'welcome back' slots for new patients — I can get you in this Thursday at 2 PM or next Tuesday at 9 AM. Which works better?" These are real slots. Block them out on the schedule for exactly this purpose.
  3. If they don't answer: text with a specific slot. "Hi [Name] — we saved a spot for you this Thursday at 2 PM with Dr. [Provider]. Just reply YES to claim it, or text us another day that works." Specific slots convert better than open-ended "let us know when you want to come in."
  4. Mail a handwritten note if no response in 48 hours. This feels excessive. It's not. New patients who receive a handwritten note reschedule at 2-3x the rate of those who get only texts and calls. The note says: "We were looking forward to meeting you. If the timing wasn't right, we'd love to try again — call [Name] directly at [Direct Line] and she'll get you in within 48 hours. — Dr. [Provider] and the [Practice] team."

This protocol recovers 25-35% of new-patient no-shows. On 50 new-patient no-shows per year, that's 12-17 patients who would have been permanently lost — now on your schedule generating revenue.

Frequently asked questions

Won't four reminders annoy patients?

Four touches sounds aggressive on paper. In practice, patients report higher satisfaction, not lower — especially when the first two touches give them an easy way to reschedule. The friction isn't the messages. The friction is showing up for an appointment they forgot about and feeling rushed and embarrassed. The 7-day and 3-day touches let them reschedule on their own time before the 24-hour window. Practices that switch from one reminder to four see fewer complaints, not more, because patients feel more in control.

What if a patient no-shows because of transportation?

This is the #1 hidden cause of no-shows in practices serving Medicare and Medicaid populations. Add a single question to your scheduling script: "Will you need any help with transportation to the appointment?" If yes, flag the appointment for a day-before confirmation call — a human call, not a text. During that call, confirm they have a ride. If they don't, offer to connect them with a medical transportation service (most Medicaid plans cover this) or help them reschedule to a day when transportation is available. This single question cuts Medicare no-show rates by 15-20% in practices that adopt it.

How is this different from automated appointment reminders in our EHR?

Most EHR reminder modules send one message — 24 or 48 hours before the appointment — and call it done. They don't do multiple touches, they don't differentiate between new patients and established patients, and they have zero follow-up after a no-show. The EHR reminder is a single net. This system is a net, a second net, a follow-up phone call, and a pattern tracker that tells you which holes in the schedule keep opening up. It's not about replacing the EHR reminder — it's about layering four additional things the EHR doesn't do.

What about same-day cancellations vs. no-shows?

Same-day cancellations are better than no-shows — they free the slot for a waitlist patient — but they still represent a gap if you don't have a waitlist. The 3-day email in the reminder sequence is designed to surface cancellations before the 24-hour window, giving you time to fill the slot. And if someone cancels same-day, they jump straight to the Strike 2 follow-up (48-hour text with a booking link) because they've already shown they want care — they just had a conflict. Don't treat cancellations the same as no-shows. A cancellation is a scheduling problem; a no-show is a relationship problem.
