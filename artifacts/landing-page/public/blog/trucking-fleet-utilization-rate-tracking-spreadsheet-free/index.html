The two hundred eighty three thousand dollar idle-truck problem

Most fleet owners track miles. They know their deadhead percentage, their loaded-mile average, their revenue per mile. But miles tell you where the truck WENT. They do not tell you where the truck WAS NOT.

Here is the math that deadhead reports miss entirely:

  • 15 trucks times 24 hours times 30 days equals 10,800 total available truck-hours per month.
  • At 65 percent utilization, trucks are only moving for 7,020 hours. The remaining 3,780 hours are idle.
  • At seventy five dollars per hour in lost revenue opportunity (conservative estimate — most fleets lose more), those 3,780 idle hours equal roughly two hundred eighty three thousand five hundred dollars in missed revenue per month.
  • That is 3.4 million dollars per year in revenue your trucks COULD have produced if they were moving instead of sitting.

And the deadhead report says everything is fine — because a sitting truck shows zero miles. Zero miles means zero deadhead. The metric that is supposed to catch fleet inefficiency is blind to its biggest component.

Deadhead tracks empty MOVING. Utilization tracks NOT MOVING. You need both. Deadhead catches the miles your trucks run without a load. Utilization catches the hours your trucks sit without moving. Together, they form the most complete fleet efficiency analysis available for free. Without utilization tracking, you are managing half of your waste problem.

The free fleet utilization tracker spreadsheet

The tracker is a Google Sheets workbook with four tabs that you can copy and start using today. No macros. No integrations. Just data entry and automatic calculations.

Tab 1: Per-Truck Daily Log

One row per truck per day. Columns include:

  • Truck ID and driver name — so you can spot driver-specific utilization patterns.
  • Hours driving loaded — the hours that generate revenue.
  • Hours driving empty — deadhead miles converted to hours (deadhead is still MOVING — it counts toward utilization).
  • Hours at shipper or receiver — detention that eats into available driving hours.
  • Hours waiting for dispatch — the single largest idle-time category for most small fleets. Dispatcher overwhelmed, load board slow, or no loads available.
  • Hours in maintenance — scheduled PMs and unscheduled repairs. A truck in the shop is an idle truck.
  • Hours driver off-duty or HOS exhausted — mandatory rest, but if it happens mid-route because of poor planning, it is a utilization problem.
  • Total utilization rate — auto-calculated: (loaded hours plus empty hours plus detention hours) divided by 24, times 100. A truck that drove 14 hours and spent 3 hours at a dock has 71 percent utilization for that day.

Tab 2: Fleet Dashboard

Auto-populated from the daily log. Shows at a glance:

  • Utilization by truck — ranked best to worst. Which three trucks are dragging down your average?
  • Utilization by day of week — are Mondays worse than Wednesdays? That tells you something about your dispatch load distribution.
  • Utilization by driver — some drivers hustle, others wait. The dashboard makes it visible.
  • Monthly trend — is utilization improving or getting worse? A line chart that updates automatically.

Tab 3: Idle-Time Root Cause Analysis

The five categories that consume your idle hours, broken down by truck and by week:

  • Dispatch lag — time between completing one load and getting assigned the next. The fix: load-board automation, pre-booked reloads, or a dedicated after-hours dispatcher.
  • Shipper or receiver delay — detention time that you may not be billing. The fix: detention tracking system (see related reading below) plus data to renegotiate with chronic offenders.
  • Maintenance — scheduled PMs are fine. Unscheduled repairs that happen during peak demand window are a scheduling problem, not a maintenance problem.
  • HOS exhaustion — driver ran out of hours before reaching a reload point. The fix: route planning that accounts for Hours of Service limits before dispatch assigns the load.
  • Dead zone or no freight — the truck is in a lane with zero backhaul demand. The fix: lane diversification or repositioning empty (which shows up as deadhead, not idle — the two metrics connect here).

The five reaons your trucks sit idle

Each root cause has a different fix. The tracker tells you WHICH root cause dominates — so you apply the right fix instead of guessing.

1. Dispatch lag — the biggest lever for small fleets

When one dispatcher manages fifteen trucks using DAT, Truckstop, and a whiteboard, the time between loads grows. A dispatcher who takes forty five minutes to find and assign a reload creates forty five minutes of idle time per truck per load. Across fifteen trucks running two loads each per day, that is twenty two hours of idle time per day — purely from dispatch lag.

The fix: Load-board automation tools that pre-filter loads by lane and rate, auto-alerts for backhauls matching your equipment, and a pre-booked reload target (every truck should have its next load assigned before it drops the current one). The dispatcher overwhelm guide in related reading covers this in depth.

2. Shipper delays — the one you can invoice

Detention at docks is the only idle-time category that can generate revenue. Most small fleets do not bill detention consistently because they do not track it consistently. The tracker logs every detention hour — so you have data to invoice, and data to identify which shippers are chronic offenders.

3. Maintenance timing — the schedule problem

A truck in for a PM during peak freight demand is a utilization loss. A truck in for an unscheduled repair because the PM was skipped to chase revenue is an even bigger utilization loss. The tracker shows maintenance hours by day — overlay that with your revenue-per-day data and you can see whether your maintenance schedule is costing you more than it is saving you.

4. HOS exhaustion — the planning failure

A driver who runs out of hours at 2 PM two hundred miles from the nearest reload is not a driver problem — it is a dispatch planning problem. The tracker flags patterns: which drivers consisently exhaust hours mid-route, and on which lanes. That data tells dispatch to stop assigning 700-mile loads to drivers with nine hours remaining.

5. Dead zones — the lane problem

Some lanes have zero backhaul demand. A truck that delivers into a dead zone will sit — or deadhead out. The utilization tracker quantifies EXACTLY how many hours each dead zone costs you per month. That number pays for the empty repositioning miles, or it tells you to stop accepting loads into that lane at current rates.

The fix-it-this-week priority list

Not all trucks contribute equally to your utilization problem. The tracker's fleet dashboard ranks trucks by idle hours. In most fleets, the three worst trucks represent roughly seventy percent of the idle-time gap. Fix those three first.

Week 1 action plan

  1. Set up the tracker — two hours. Enter last week's data to establish a baseline utilization rate.
  2. Identify your worst three trucks — the ones with the most idle hours. The dashboard auto-ranks them.
  3. Root-cause each one — is the problem dispatch lag, shipper delay, or something else? The root cause analysis tab tells you.
  4. Apply ONE fix per truck — do not try to fix everything at once. If truck 14 is idle because of dispatch lag, assign the dispathcer a dedicated reload-search window for that truck.
  5. Re-measure Friday afternoon — did utilization improve? The weekly trend chart shows you.

Frequenly asked questions

What is a good fleet utilization rate for a small trucking company?

A good fleet utilization rate for a small trucking fleet is 80 to 85 percent — meaning trucks are moving for 80 to 85 percent of available hours. Below 75 percent indicates a serious idle-time problem. The national average for small fleets is roughly 65 to 70 percent, which means most fleet owners are losing twenty to thirty percent of potential revenue to idle trucks. Every percentage point improvement on a 15-truck fleet is worth roughly four thousand three hundred dollars per month.

How is fleet utilization rate calculated for trucking?

Fleet utilization rate equals total hours trucks spent driving (loaded plus empty) divided by total available hours, multiplied by 100. Available hours equal number of trucks times 24 hours times days in the period. For example: a 15-truck fleet with 10,800 available hours in a 30-day month where trucks drove 7,020 hours has a 65 percent utilization rate. The remaining 3,780 hours are idle.

What is the difference between deadhead tracking and utilization tracking?

Deadhead tracking measures EMPTY MILES — how many miles trucks run without a load generating revenue. Utilization tracking measures IDLE HOURS — how many hours trucks sit not moving at all. A truck sitting in the yard for eight hours shows zero miles and is invisible to deadhead reports. You need both metrics for complete fleet efficiency visibility.

How much does fleet utilization software cost for a small trucking company?

Commercial fleet utilization and telematics software costs two hundred to eight hundred dollars per month per truck. For a 15-truck fleet, that is three thousand to twelve thousand dollars per month. The free spreadsheet in this guide costs zero dollars and takes roughly two hours to set up, providing the same analysis — per-truck dashboards, root cause breakdowns, and decision matrixes — without the monthly subscription.

What should I fix first — deadhead or utilization?

Almost always utilization. A one percent improvement in utilization on a 15-truck fleet recovers roughly four thousand three hundred dollars per month. Deadhead improvement typically yields less per percentage point. The utilization-versus-deadhead matrix in this guide tells you exactly which to fix based on your specific numbers. For most small fleets with utilization below 75 percent, fixing idle time generates faster ROI than chasing empty miles.
