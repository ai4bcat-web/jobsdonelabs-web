12 Hours a Week on Prior Auths? Free Time & Cost Tracker to Prove It

By Ryne Bandolik · Jobs Done Labs · November 19, 2026

Key takeaways

Most small practices cannot tell you how many hours prior authorizations consume — and without data, you cannot make the business case for change. This free 2-week PA time audit spreadsheet gives you a defensible number. Track every PA by payer, procedure, and staff member. At the end of two weeks, you will know exactly which payers are the worst offenders and what a PA specialist or electronic PA solution would save you.

You Know Prior Auths Are Eating Your Staff Alive. But You Cannot Prove It.

Every practice manager knows the feeling. Your best medical assistant spends 45 minutes on hold with UnitedHealthcare for a single MRI authorization. Your front desk coordinator gets pulled off patient check-in three times a day to fax prior auth paperwork. Your office manager stays late every Tuesday reconciling the PA log against the appointment schedule to figure out which procedures are still pending.

But when the practice owner asks "how bad is it really?" — you do not have a number. You have a gut feeling and a stack of fax confirmations.

Gut feelings do not justify hiring a dedicated PA specialist at 22 dollars an hour plus benefits. Gut feelings do not convince anyone to switch to electronic prior authorization at 300 dollars per month per provider. Gut feelings do not make the business case.

Data does.

The reality most practices do not measure: A typical small practice with 3-5 providers spends 12-16 staff hours per week on prior authorizations. At 20 dollars per hour, that is 12,480-16,640 dollars per year — for one staff member. Many practices have multiple people touching PAs, and the real cost is higher because PA work displaces revenue-generating tasks.

The 4 Numbers Every Practice Should Track

You do not need a complex time-study with consultants and stopwatches. You need four numbers, tracked consistently for two weeks:

The 4-number PA audit

  1. PA requests per week — total volume by procedure type (imaging, specialist referral, medication, DME). Volume alone often surprises practice owners who assume it is "maybe 10 a week" when the real number is 30-40.
  2. Average minutes per PA — how long does one PA actually take from first call to confirmation? Include hold time, callbacks, faxing, portal logins, and re-submissions. Most practices guess 10 minutes. The real average is often 18-25 minutes.
  3. Denial rate by payer — which payers deny PAs most often, forcing rework and appeals? One payer with a 40% initial denial rate creates 3x the work of a payer with a 10% denial rate for the same procedure volume.
  4. Staff hours consumed — when you multiply PA volume by minutes per PA and divide by 60, what is the weekly total? This is the number that goes to the practice owner.

How to Run a 2-Week PA Time Audit

The audit is simple but the timing matters. Here is the proven sequence:

Week 1: Log everything without changing anything

Week 1 is your baseline. Tell staff to log every PA exactly as they do it today — same methods, same workflows, same payer portals. Do not try to be more efficient. Do not shortcut. The goal is a clean baseline of what PA actually costs when nobody is watching.

Common Week 1 discoveries: staff realize they spend way more time on PAs than they thought (the act of logging makes it visible for the first time). One practice discovered their "quick 5-minute PA check" was actually 18 minutes every time because of hold times they had stopped noticing.

Week 2: Capture the variation

Week 2 smooths out the anomalies. If Week 1 had an unusually heavy Monday (three complex imaging PAs that each took 45 minutes), Week 2 shows whether that is normal or a fluke. By the end of Week 2, you have a reliable weekly average.

At the end of Week 2, open the Payer Analytics tab. Sort by "total hours consumed." The top 2-3 payers are your priority targets.

What the data typically reveals: In most practices, 2-3 payers consume 60-70% of total PA time. The rest are relatively fast. This means you do not need to fix the entire PA process — you just need to fix the relationship with those 2-3 payers. Switch to electronic PA, assign a dedicated staff member to handle only those payers, or build a business case for outsourcing PAs for those specific carriers.

What the Data Will Tell You (Real Patterns)

After two weeks, the spreadsheet will surface patterns you cannot see day-to-day:

  • "Payer X takes 3x longer than Payer Y." — Now you can switch to electronic PA specifically for Payer X, or negotiate with your clearinghouse for better PA routing. You are not guessing — you have a number.
  • "We are spending 16 hours a week on PAs." — That is a 0.4 FTE. At 22 dollars per hour, a dedicated PA specialist costs about 45,760 dollars per year. But if PAs are consuming 0.4 FTE of a 25 dollar per hour medical assistant's time, plus fragments of the office manager's time, plus the billing coordinator's time — the real cost is higher, and the business case for hiring a PA specialist writes itself.
  • "Denials from Payer Z waste 5 hours a week in rework." — If Payer Z denies 40% of initial PAs, every approved PA from Payer Z actually costs 2.5x the logged time because of appeals and resubmissions. This is a contract negotiation point at renewal.
  • "Mondays and Tuesdays are the worst." — PAs stack up over the weekend. If you batch PA work on Monday mornings, you are creating a bottleneck. Shift PA initiation to Thursday/Friday for procedures scheduled the following week and Monday becomes manageable.
