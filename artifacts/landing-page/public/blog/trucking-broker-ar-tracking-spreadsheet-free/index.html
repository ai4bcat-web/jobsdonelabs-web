The Hidden Cost of "Trust Me" Broker Payment Tracking

Here's a story that replays in every small fleet. You run 6 trucks and work with 14 brokers. A factoring company sends you a statement every couple of weeks, and you assume it's the full picture. Then one quarter you find a $3,400 load TQL short-paid — they deducted a $175 lumper fee you never approved — and by the time you notice, it's 90 days later and they refuse to reverse it.

The problem isn't that the factoring company is lying. It's that the factor only tracks the invoices they funded. What falls through the cracks is everything else:

  • Short-pays under $200. A $175 lumper deduction here, a $90 fuel-surcharge haircut there — individually invisible, collectively real money, and below the factor's attention threshold.
  • Unauthorized deductions. Lumper fees, detention clawbacks, and "processing fees" that never appeared on the rate confirmation you signed.
  • Slow-pay patterns. Which brokers consistently pay at day 45 instead of day 30, and which ones pay at day 75.
  • Brokers who "forget" to process rate confirmations. The load was delivered, the POD is clean, but the rate con was never entered on their side — and nobody on either end notices for six weeks.

The fix is a carrier-side accounts receivable tracker: who owes your trucking company money, by broker, with short-pay detection — independent of whatever your factor happens to fund. This is the one gap in a trucking back office that's otherwise well-covered. We've written systems for unpaid detention, fuel card abuse, and the shipper side of freight bill audit — but nothing tracks the money coming in from brokers. Until now.

The Free Carrier Accounts Receivable Spreadsheet

The tracker is a free Google Sheets workbook with three tabs. It's built for the reality of a small fleet — not the chart-of-accounts abstraction of a general ledger, and not the 2-week lag of a factoring statement.

Tab 1 — Per-broker AR

  • Broker & contact — name, AP contact, MC number, and payment terms.
  • Total outstanding — every open invoice summed, so you know exactly how much each broker owes right now.
  • Oldest unpaid invoice — the single number that tells you whether this is a normal lag or a problem.
  • Avg days-to-pay (12-month) — their actual behavior, not their stated terms. A broker whose terms say net-30 but pays in 48 days gets flagged.
  • Open short-pay disputes — a running list with the amount in question and the status.
  • Reliability rating — the auto-calculated score (see below).

Tab 2 — Per-load log

  • Load number, broker, rate-con amount, invoice number.
  • Dates — delivered, invoiced, due, paid.
  • Amount paid vs. amount owed — the difference is your short-pay.
  • Factoring company & dispute status — so you can reconcile against the factor's statement when the numbers drift apart.

Tab 3 — Dashboard

  • Total AR and AR by broker.
  • Aging buckets — current, 30, 60, 90+ days.
  • Weekly cash-in projection — what's actually due to land this week, so you can plan fuel, payroll, and truck payments.
  • Brokers ranked by reliability — the list that tells you who to book next and who to demand quick-pay from.
  • Auto-highlight — an invoice turns YELLOW at net+7 (courtesy check-in) and RED at net+30 (formal demand).

The design rule is the same one behind our load confirmation filing system: the rate confirmation is the source document. Every row on the per-load tab should be traceable back to a signed rate con, because when a broker short-pays, the rate con plus the POD is the entire dispute.

The "Before You Take the Next Load" Broker Reliability Score

This is the part that changes behavior, not just bookkeeping. The score is auto-calculated from three inputs, weighted by recency:

Input What It Measures Why It Matters
Days-to-pay Average time from invoice to payment over 12 months. Slow payment is free financing for them and a cash squeeze for you.
Short-pay frequency How often the amount paid is less than the rate-con amount. Short-payers don't change; they just get better at hiding it.
Dispute resolution How quickly and fairly they resolve a flagged short-pay. A broker that fixes a $175 error in a week is worth keeping. One that stonewalls is a liability.

A broker that lands in the RED — slow pay, frequent short-pays, hostile disputes — triggers a rule: quick-pay or COD before the next load. You don't fire them on emotion; you re-term them on data. This single feature prevents the majority of bad-debt loads before they're ever booked, because the worst brokers reveal themselves in 90 days of payment history.

The 5-Minute Short-Pay Dispute Process

Short-pays are recoverable — but only if you catch them fast. The window matters because a broker that short-pays you $175 and gets a polite email on day three will usually just cut the check. The same $175 on day ninety is a refusal, because by then the load is closed on their side and the person who short-paid it has moved on.

The process, start to finish

  • Day 1 — spot it. The per-load tab flags the short-pay the week it happens. Mark it "open dispute."
  • Day 3 — follow up. A short email to the broker's AP contact: rate con, POD, and the exact dollar amount of the short-pay. No anger, just the documents.
  • Day 7 — formal dispute. If there's no response, escalate with the rate con and POD attached and a specific figure owed, cc'ing the factoring company if one is involved.

The $175 lumper deduction in the opening story is the test case: caught in week one, it's an easy reversal. Caught at day ninety, it's gone. Your tracker's job is to make "week one" the default, not the exception.

When to Fire a Broker (With Data, Not Emotion)

Every fleet owner has a broker they feel is slow to pay. The reliability score turns that feeling into a 12-month report. Once a quarter, sort the per-broker tab by score and look at the bottom three.

The math that makes firing worth it

Shifting average days-to-pay from 52 to 34 across a 6-truck fleet accelerates $12,000-$18,000 of cash flow — money that's no longer sitting in someone else's bank account for an extra three weeks. Firing or re-terming your bottom three brokers, and demanding quick-pay from the borderline ones, is a cash-flow decision, not a grudge.

The conversation is simple when you have the data: "Over the last 12 months you've averaged 54 days to pay and short-paid on 9 loads. We'll keep hauling for you, but going forward it's quick-pay or COD." Some brokers fix it on the spot. The ones that argue are the ones you're better off without.

When to Graduate to a TMS

The spreadsheet has a ceiling, and it's worth being honest about it:

Fleet size Right tool for AR Why
Under 10 trucks Free spreadsheet Manual entry is manageable and the flexibility beats an under-used TMS.
10-25 trucks Entry TMS (Rose Rocket, ITS Dispatch, AscendTMS, Axon) Hundreds of invoices a month make manual entry error-prone; a factoring or AR module pays for itself.
25+ trucks Full TMS Manual reconciliation simply doesn't scale; the AR module is table stakes.

The deciding signal is time, not a round truck count: when reconciliation eats 3+ hours a week and errors start creeping in, upgrade. Until then, the spreadsheet isn't a stopgap — it's the right tool.

Frequently asked questions

How do trucking companies track broker payments?

Most small fleets rely on the factoring company's statement, which is the wrong place to look: the factor only tracks the invoices THEY funded, and their statement is typically two weeks behind. A proper carrier AR tracker is built the other way around — one tab per broker showing total outstanding, the oldest unpaid invoice, average days-to-pay over 12 months, and open short-pay disputes; plus a per-load tab with the rate-confirmation amount, invoiced date, amount paid, and any short-pay. The spreadsheet highlights an invoice YELLOW at net+7 days and RED at net+30, so you follow up before a 45-day term quietly becomes 75.

What's the best free accounts receivable spreadsheet for small trucking fleets?

A free Google Sheets workbook with two tabs. The per-broker tab lists every broker with their contact, MC number, payment terms, total outstanding, oldest unpaid invoice, 12-month average days-to-pay, open short-pay disputes, and a reliability score. The per-load tab is one row per load: load number, broker, rate-confirmation amount, invoice number, dates, amount paid, short-pay amount, factoring company, and dispute status. A dashboard tab sums total AR, breaks it down by broker, buckets it by age, and projects weekly cash-in. Conditional formatting flags past-due invoices so nothing falls through the cracks. It handles fleets up to roughly 15-20 trucks before a TMS makes more sense.

How do I dispute a freight broker short-pay?

Dispute it fast and with documents. The 5-minute process: Day 1, spot the short-pay in your AR tracker the week it happens and flag it. Day 3, send a short follow-up email to the broker's accounts-payable contact with the rate confirmation, the POD, and the exact amount of the short-pay. Day 7, if there's no resolution, send a formal dispute with the rate-con and POD attached and a specific dollar figure owed. The key is catching it in week one — a $175 lumper deduction you never approved is recoverable on day three and usually refused on day ninety.

How long should a trucking company wait for broker payment before following up?

Don't wait at all past the agreed term. Most broker agreements are net-30 to net-45, so your tracker should flag an invoice YELLOW at net+7 (a courtesy check-in) and RED at net+30 (a formal demand). A single polite follow-up at net+7 recovers a surprising share of "forgotten" invoices, and it's far cheaper than discovering at day 90 that the broker never processed the rate confirmation. The rule of thumb: if payment is more than a week past the agreed term, you should already have an email thread open.

Which freight brokers pay fastest?

That's a question your own data should answer, not a public list — payment speed varies by broker, by lane, and over time. That's exactly why the reliability score in the AR tracker matters: it calculates each broker's score from 12 months of days-to-pay, short-pay frequency, and dispute resolution, then ranks them. A broker that averages 52 days and short-pays often gets a RED score, and you demand quick-pay or COD before taking their next load. A broker that pays in 25 days without disputes gets a GREEN score and first call on your trucks.

When should a trucking company use TMS software for AR instead of spreadsheets?

Below roughly 10 trucks, a free spreadsheet is the right tool and often more flexible than a TMS you're not fully using. Between 10 and 25 trucks, an entry-level TMS (Rose Rocket, ITS Dispatch, AscendTMS, Axon) with a factoring or AR module starts to pay for itself because manual entry across hundreds of invoices a month produces its own errors. Above 25 trucks, a full TMS is close to mandatory. The deciding signal is time, not truck count: when reconciliation takes 3+ hours a week and errors are creeping in, it's time to upgrade.
