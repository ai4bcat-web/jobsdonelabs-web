14 Months Ago a Patient Signed Your $50/Month Payment Agreement. She Hasn't Paid Since — and You Just Found Out. Here's the Free Tracking Spreadsheet That Makes Sure That Never Happens Again.

By Ryne Bandolik · August 30, 2026 · Healthcare & Medical Practices

Your front desk hands every self-pay patient a one-page "Payment Agreement" form. The patient signs it, the front desk makes a copy, and the original goes into a manila folder in the filing cabinet. That folder now holds 47 active payment agreements — some dating back 18 months. Nobody has opened it since March. Three patients have missed 5+ consecutive payments. One owes $1,400 that will never be collected because nobody noticed until the balance was 14 months old. And your practice manager just discovered it while looking for a W-9.

This isn't negligence. It's structural. Your practice management system tracks insurance AR beautifully — claim status, payer response, EOB posting. But patient payment plans? They live outside the system entirely. The PM software wasn't built for "Maria owes $50/month for 12 months starting June 3rd — is she paying?" So your staff bridges the gap with paper, and paper has no memory.

The $22,000 invisible patient AR problem Medical practices collect roughly 94% of insurance AR within 90 days. Patient AR? The collection rate drops to 40% after 90 days, and to under 15% after 120 days. A 3-provider practice with 200 active payment agreements averaging $400 each carries $80,000 in patient AR at any moment. If 60% of balances over 90 days are never collected, that's $22,000 in permanent write-offs — every year — from agreements that were perfectly good when they were signed. The spreadsheet system below catches every overdue payment before it hits the 90-day cliff and costs $0.

Why your practice management system can't track patient payment plans (and what to do about it)

Your PM system handles insurance billing because the workflow is standardized: submit claim, receive EOB, post payment, appeal denial. Patient payment plans, by contrast, are a custom arrangement every time — $50/month for 10 months, $75/month for 8 months, $125/month for 6 months, sometimes with a down payment, sometimes interest-free, sometimes with a payment holiday in December when the patient asked nicely. There's no standard EDI transaction for "Maria paid $40 instead of $50 this month and promised to catch up next month."

The spreadsheet bridges the gap between what your PM system does (post individual payments) and what you need to know (is this agreement on track, behind, or abandoned?). It's not a replacement for your PM system — it's the reconciliation layer that your PM system was never designed to include.

The tracking system walkthrough: 4 tabs that replace the filing cabinet

Build this system once — about 45 minutes — and your monthly patient AR review goes from "flip through 47 paper folders and make notes on a legal pad" to "open a spreadsheet, sort by days overdue, and hand a one-page call list to your front desk."

Tab 3: Monthly AR Dashboard

This tab pulls from the Active Agreements Ledger and Payment Log. Update takes 30 seconds — refresh the formulas and you have a practice-owner-ready snapshot. Give this to your practice owner the first Monday of every month.

  • Active agreements: count of rows in Tab 1 where Status ≠ "Paid in Full"
  • Total patient AR outstanding: SUM of Balance Remaining (Col H) for active agreements
  • Total collected this month: SUM of Amount Paid in Tab 2 for payments dated this month
  • Agreements current (<30 days since last payment): count where Col J < 30
  • Agreements 30–59 days late: count where Col J is 30–59
  • Agreements 60–89 days late: count where Col J is 60–89
  • Agreements 90+ days late — COLLECTION RISK: count where Col J ≥ 90. These balances have a <15% probability of collection. Flag for immediate action or write-off decision.
  • Largest single overdue balance: MAX of Balance Remaining for agreements 30+ days late
  • Underpayments this month: count of rows in Tab 2 where Shortfall > $0

Tab 4: Paid / Closed Agreements

When an agreement is paid in full — or written off, sent to collections, or the patient transfers out — cut the row from Tab 1 and paste it here. Add a "Closure Reason" column (Paid in Full, Transferred Out, Deceased, Sent to Collections, Written Off — Uncollectible). This is your audit trail for year-end AR reporting and for defending write-off decisions if your accountant asks.

The overdue patient call script: collect money without sounding like a collections agency

These are your patients — people who came to you for care and who, in most cases, intended to pay. They got busy, they forgot, they had an unexpected expense, or they assumed the practice would bill them (even though the agreement says they're responsible). The call isn't adversarial — it's a reminder from someone they trust.

The 90-day cliff is real — here's the math Patient balances under 30 days have an 85% collection probability. At 60 days, it drops to 55%. At 90 days, it's under 15%. The difference between catching a $400 balance at day 35 vs day 95 is roughly $280 in actual collected dollars — per account. For a practice with 47 active agreements, the monthly 15-minute dashboard review prevents an average of $1,800 in permanent write-offs every single month.

How to handle the three most common patient payment problems

1. "I can't afford the $50 this month — can I pay $25?"

Say yes. Document it. A patient who pays $25/month for 20 months is infinitely better than a patient who stops paying entirely because $50 feels impossible. In Tab 2 (Payment Log), enter $25 and note "reduced to $25/mo per patient request 8/15" in Tab 1 Col N. The agreement isn't broken — it's modified. The spreadsheet tracks both the original terms and the actual payment history, so you always know what's happening.

2. The patient who sends sporadic payments with no pattern

Some patients pay $100 one month, $0 the next two, $75 the month after. The Total Collected (Col G) vs Balance Remaining (Col H) tells you whether they're roughly on track — even if the month-to-month is chaotic. As long as the agreement is moving toward zero over a reasonable timeframe, don't micromanage the payment cadence. Flag them only when 90+ days pass with zero payments.

3. "I switched insurance — why am I still getting bills?"

This patient had a deductible or non-covered service when they signed the agreement. Their new insurance doesn't change the past balance. Explain it clearly: "The agreement covers services you received on [date] when you had [old insurance]. Your new plan is great — but it doesn't apply retroactively. Your balance from that visit is still [amount]." Document the conversation in Tab 1 Col N. If the patient refuses to pay, that's a practice-policy decision (write-off vs collections) — but at least you know exactly what's happening, which the filing cabinet system never told you.

When to upgrade from the spreadsheet

The spreadsheet system works for practices managing fewer than 200 active payment agreements. Beyond that — or if you're experiencing any of these — consider dedicated automation:

  • 200+ active payment agreements. At this volume, the monthly 15-minute review stretches to 45+ minutes, and tracking individual patient communications becomes unwieldy.
  • Multiple locations or providers with separate agreement workflows. If each provider handles payment plans differently, a unified system with role-based access and standardized workflows eliminates inconsistency.
  • You're sending patient statements manually. If someone on your team spends 3–5 hours per month printing, folding, and mailing patient statements, automation can generate and send them automatically — saving that time entirely.
  • You've had a patient dispute a balance and couldn't produce documentation. If you've ever been in a situation where a patient said "I already paid that" and you couldn't prove otherwise because the paper folder was incomplete, you need timestamped, audit-ready payment tracking.
