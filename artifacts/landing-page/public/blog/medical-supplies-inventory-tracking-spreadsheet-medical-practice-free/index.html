The 30-day expiry alert — stop finding expired stock by accident

The core formula in the Expiry Dashboard tab is deceptively simple:

=IF(days_until_expiry<30,"⚠️ USE OR LOSE",IF(days_until_expiry<60,"⚠️ PRIORITY — use before newer stock","OK"))

This one formula replaces the "nobody noticed" system your practice has been running on. Monday morning, sort the Expiry Dashboard by the alert column. Every "USE OR LOSE" item gets addressed before Friday.

Here's what addressing a flagged item actually means:

  • Vaccines with 30-60 days remaining: Prioritize in the appointment schedule. Move these lots to the front of the fridge with a "USE FIRST" sticker. Alert providers: "We have 12 doses of lot XXXX expiring March 15 — please use these before lot YYYY when both are clinically appropriate."
  • Vaccines with <30 days: If unopened, contact the manufacturer or distributor about return eligibility (McKesson accepts returns on unexpired product within 30 days of expiration for most vaccines). If return isn't possible, write off and document the lot number/loss for your inventory reconciliation. The financial loss hurts — but it's $240 for 40 doses you catch at day 28 vs $240 for 40 doses you discover expired 6 weeks ago AND now have to explain to your accountant why inventory doesn't match the purchase orders.
  • Medications with <60 days: Rotate to the front of the medication cabinet. If it's a sample medication from a drug rep, check whether the rep will swap unexpired stock (most will — it's part of their quarterly visit routine).
  • PPE/clinical supplies with <60 days: These have longer shelf lives but the alert still matters — it means you're carrying too much stock or someone ordered a bulk shipment when the par level was fine. Adjust the par level down for next quarter.

Flu season adjustment rule: From October 1 through March 31, double the par level for any item whose consumption spikes during respiratory season. Set a calendar reminder for September 15 to review and update seasonal pars. Set another for April 1 to dial them back down — otherwise you'll order flu-season quantities in May and have a year's supply of swabs gathering dust.

Vaccine ordering protocol: Order flu vaccine in 3 waves, not one bulk shipment. Wave 1 (September delivery): 40% of projected season doses — covers early demand and ensures you have stock when patients start asking. Wave 2 (October delivery): 30% — peak demand buffer. Wave 3 (November delivery): 30% — late-season and walk-ins. This reduces the risk of carrying 200 expired doses in April because you over-ordered in August. The spreadsheet tracks doses by lot and wave — you'll know exactly how many doses from each wave are still on hand at any point.

How to handle a vaccine fridge failure

One power outage. One fridge door left ajar overnight. One compressor failure on a Saturday when nobody's in the office until Monday. The contents of that fridge — $5,000-$15,000 in vaccines — are now in limbo. Here's the protocol (print this and tape it to the fridge):

  1. Do NOT discard anything. The vaccines may still be viable. Manufacturer stability data often shows efficacy for days or weeks outside of recommended storage — but only the manufacturer can authorize use after a temperature excursion.
  2. Quarantine immediately. Move all affected vials to a separate, temperature-monitored container. Label clearly: "QUARANTINED — DO NOT USE — Temperature excursion [date/time]." Do NOT mix quarantined vials with unaffected stock.
  3. Check the temperature log. What was the min/max temperature during the excursion? How long was the fridge out of range? The manufacturer will need: make/model of fridge, duration of excursion, min and max temperatures reached, and lot numbers of all affected product.
  4. Call the manufacturer (not the distributor). McKesson and Henry Schein distribute vaccines but don't make them. Look up the manufacturer's medical affairs line (Sanofi Pasteur: 1-800-VACCINE, Merck: 1-800-672-6372, GSK: 1-888-825-5249, Pfizer: 1-800-438-1985). Tell them exactly what happened — they'll reference their stability data and tell you which lots are safe to use and which must be discarded.
  5. Document everything. If you file an insurance claim for spoiled vaccine inventory, you'll need: date/time of excursion, temperature log data, manufacturer's written determination, list of affected lot numbers and quantities, and the replacement value. The spreadsheet's Vaccine Inventory tab has all of this pre-organized — export to PDF, attach to the claim, done.
  6. Replace the fridge. If the failure was equipment-related (compressor, seal, thermostat), replace the unit. A new medical-grade refrigerator costs $1,500-$3,000. One excursion that destroys $10,000 in vaccines costs $10,000. The math is not complicated.

How do I handle vaccine lot recalls?

When a manufacturer issues a vaccine lot recall, you need to answer three questions in under 30 minutes: (1) Do I have any product from that lot? (2) Where is it — which fridge, which shelf? (3) How many doses do I have, and have any been administered? The Vaccine Inventory tab answers all three in one lookup. Search by lot number — every vial from that lot appears with its location, doses received, doses administered, doses remaining, and temperature history. Quarantine the remaining doses immediately, report the lot status to the manufacturer per their recall instructions, and document the disposition. Without lot-level tracking, a recall means pulling every vial from every fridge, reading every label, and hoping you didn't administer any recalled doses — a 2-3 hour panic that the spreadsheet turns into a 5-minute search.

How do I set par levels if I've never tracked usage before?

If you don't have historical consumption data, start with a 4-week manual count. Every Monday for 4 weeks, count every item and log the current on-hand quantity. At the end of the month, calculate: average weekly usage = (Week1 starting count — Week4 ending count + any orders received) ÷ 4. Set par level = (average weekly usage × vendor lead time in weeks) + 20% safety buffer. After 3 months of tracking, adjust pars based on actual consumption patterns. If an item triggers the reorder alert more than twice between scheduled orders, raise the par. If an item sits above par for 8+ weeks, lower it. The spreadsheet tracks reorder frequency automatically in the Reorder Queue tab — you'll see patterns within the first quarter.
