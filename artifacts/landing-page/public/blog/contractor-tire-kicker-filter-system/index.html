You Drove 240 Miles to Give 7 Free Estimates Last Week — And 5 of Those People Were Never Going to Buy

By Ryne Bandolik · Jobs Done Labs · July 29, 2026

"I swear to God, I just drove 45 minutes to a kitchen remodel estimate and the guy had no budget, no timeline, and his wife wasn't even home. He was just 'curious what it might cost.'" That's from a real r/Contractor thread, July 2026 — and if you run a trade, you've lived some version of it every single week.

The numbers are brutal. A mid-size plumbing or HVAC shop sends someone to 4-6 estimates per week. Depending on your market, 40-60% of those go to tire-kickers — people collecting 3+ bids without real intent, or with no budget, or who are 6 months from pulling the trigger. Each dead estimate costs 30-60 minutes of windshield time, 30-45 minutes on site, and 20-30 minutes writing a proposal — roughly 1.5 hours you'll never bill. At 5 dead estimates a week and $75/hour, that's $562/week in lost labor alone. Over a year: $29,250. Add $0.65/mile vehicle cost and it pushes past $40K.

And that's just the direct cost. The real damage is the opportunity cost: every hour spent in a tire-kicker's kitchen is an hour you're not selling to a ready buyer, not managing your crew, and not running your business.

The reframe: You don't need to stop giving free estimates. You need to stop giving them to people who were never going to hire you. A $0 pre-qualification system that filters just half the dead estimates recovers $15,000-20,000/year — without spending a dime on software.

The math: what tire-kickers actually cost you

Run your own numbers. Grab last week:

  • Count the estimates. How many did you (or your estimator) drive to?
  • Count the wins. How many turned into signed contracts?
  • Estimate the dead ones. Wins minus total = dead estimates. Multiply by 1.5 hours and your loaded hourly rate. That's what you burned last week on people who were never going to buy.

Example: 12 estimates, 4 wins = 8 dead. 8 × 1.5 hours × $75/hour = $900 in one week. Annualized: $46,800. Before you even count the truck, the gas, and the proposals you wrote at 9pm.

The Tire-Kicker Filter: 3 parts, $0, built this week

There are only three pieces. None requires software. All can be running by Friday.

Part 1 — The 5-question phone script

When a lead calls or texts, someone runs these five questions before anything goes on the calendar:

  1. Budget range. "Most [project type] jobs like yours land between $X and $Y. Does that fit what you had in mind?" If they hesitate, choke, or say "we're just trying to get a sense" — they're price-shopping. Give them a ballpark range and ask them to call back when they have a number. You just saved an hour.
  2. Timeline. "Are you looking to start in the next 30-60 days, or just gathering ideas?" "Just gathering ideas" goes into a follow-up list — not onto your calendar. You'll circle back in 60 days. Right now, you've got real buyers to see.
  3. Decision-maker. "Will everyone who's part of the decision be there when I come out?" One walkthrough, one decision. Not a re-do for the spouse, not "let me talk to my partner and I'll get back to you." If they can't get everyone in the room, they're not ready.
  4. Scope reality. Two questions that confirm it's a job type you actually want and can price — e.g. "Is this a full gut or a cosmetic refresh?" for remodelers, or "What's the square footage?" for flooring. Nothing complex — just enough to confirm the job is real.
  5. How they found you. "Just curious — did someone refer you, or did you find us online?" A referral passes the gate automatically. A Google search who found three other contractors and is collecting bids? Flag them for a follow-up — but maybe not a same-week site visit.

The gate: Leads who pass 4+ questions get a slot. Leads who pass 2-3 get a ballpark range and a follow-up. Leads who pass 0-1 get a polite "reach back out when you're ready." The script takes 4 minutes and saves you 90 — every single time it filters a tire-kicker.

Part 2 — The automated SMS qualifying sequence

Not every lead calls. Many come through your website, Facebook, or text message. For those, use this 3-message SMS sequence — set it up once in your phone or an auto-responder, and it runs itself:

  1. Message 1 (instant auto-reply): "Hey [name], thanks for reaching out! Quick one — most [project type] jobs like yours land around $X-Y. Does that work for your budget? Just want to make sure we're in the same ballpark before I send someone out."
  2. Message 2 (if they reply yes): "Great. Two more quick questions: (1) Are you looking to start in the next 30 days or just gathering info? (2) Will everyone who makes the decision be there for the walkthrough?"
  3. Message 3 (routing): If they pass both — "Perfect. Here's my calendar link to grab a time that works. Looking forward to it." If they're gathering info or can't get everyone — "No worries at all. Here's a rough ballpark for what you described, based on similar projects we've done. When you're closer to pulling the trigger, reach back out and we'll get you on the schedule."

The SMS sequence filters 30-40% of tire-kickers before anyone even answers the phone. It runs while you're on a jobsite. It costs nothing. And it makes sure the leads on your calendar have already passed the first gate.

Part 3 — The minimum-job threshold framework

There's a dollar amount below which a free on-site estimate doesn't make financial sense. Find yours:

  • Your estimate cost: Average drive + walkthrough + proposal time (hours) × your loaded hourly rate
  • Your target margin: What percentage of the job price do you need to keep after costs?
  • Your threshold: Estimate cost ÷ (1 - target margin) = minimum job size for a free site visit

Example: 1.5 hours at $75/hr = $112.50. Target margin = 40%. Threshold = $112.50 ÷ 0.6 = $187.50. Round up to a practical number — most trades land between $2,500 and $5,000.

Jobs under your threshold get a ballpark range — text them an estimate based on photos and past jobs. It takes 10 minutes instead of 90, and the margins still work because you never left the office. Jobs over your threshold get the full site visit — and now every visit goes to a lead who's worth the drive.

This isn't turning away small jobs. It's protecting the margin on them. A $1,200 water heater quoted from photos in 10 minutes has great margin. A $1,200 water heater with a 90-minute free estimate has almost none. You still do the work — you just don't give away the estimate.

The dead-estimate cost calculator

Know exactly what each dead estimate costs you so you can decide what's worth filtering:

Plug in your numbers

  1. Your loaded hourly rate: Wage + payroll taxes + workers' comp + benefits ÷ hours worked. For most trades this lands between $55-95/hour.
  2. Average estimate round-trip miles: Use 30-50 if you're not sure. Multiply by your per-mile cost (IRS rate is $0.70/mile for 2026).
  3. Your close rate on unqualified leads: Be honest. Most contractors converting walk-in leads without qualification close 20-30%.
  4. Dead estimates per week: Total estimates × (1 - close rate).
  5. Annual cost: Dead estimates × (labor cost + vehicle cost) × 52 weeks + lost opportunity.

For a shop doing 12 estimates/week at 25% close rate with $75/hr loaded cost and 40-mile round trips: 9 dead estimates × ($112.50 + $26.00) × 52 = $64,818/year in wasted estimate costs. Filter half with a $0 phone script: $32,409 recovered — before you count the extra jobs you closed with the time you got back.

How do I pre-qualify leads before driving out to give an estimate?

If they choke on the low end, they're not your customer. (2) Timeline — "Are you looking to start in the next 30-60 days, or just gathering ideas?" Should I charge for estimates to filter out tire-kickers?

Charging for estimates CAN work — but only after you've fixed the qualification process. The right sequence: first, install the 5-question phone screen so you only estimate for qualified leads. Your close rate will climb and dead-estimate hours will drop without scaring anyone off. Then, implement the minimum-job threshold: any project under $X (typically $2,500-5,000 depending on your trade) gets a ballpark range by text, not a site visit. Finally — and only then — charge a proposal fee for full site visits on larger jobs. Credit the fee toward the project if they sign. That way, the fee filters the last tier of tire-kickers without scaring off serious buyers. Charging before you qualify is like putting a locked gate on your driveway before you've paved the road — you'll lose good customers along with the bad ones.

What's a minimum-job-threshold and how do I set one?

A minimum-job threshold is the dollar amount below which a free, on-site estimate doesn't make financial sense — the point where the cost of your time and truck exceeds the expected margin on the job. To find yours: take your average drive + walkthrough + proposal time (typically 1-2 hours), multiply by your loaded hourly cost (wage + burden + vehicle), and divide by your target gross margin percentage. For most trades, this lands between $2,500 and $5,000. Any lead under that number gets a ballpark range via text or email instead of a site visit. This isn't turning away small jobs — it's protecting your margin on them. A $1,200 water heater replacement with a free 90-minute estimate at $75/hour loaded cost leaves almost nothing. A $1,200 replacement quoted from photos in 10 minutes still has healthy margin. The threshold is about where you estimate, not whether you do the work.
