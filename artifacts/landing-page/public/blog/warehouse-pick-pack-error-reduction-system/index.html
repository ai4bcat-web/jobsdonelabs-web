Pick/Pack Errors Cost You $54,000 Last Year

You pulled up your returns report last month and saw numbers you didn't want to believe. Wrong items shipped. Wrong quantities. Customer got someone else's order entirely. Each one cost you $15–35 in return shipping and replacement — plus the customer who never reordered. And you didn't even count the ones you never heard about because the customer just quietly left.

If you search "how to reduce picking errors in warehouse" right now, the #1 result is a Reddit thread. Not a guide. Not a solution. A Reddit thread where warehouse operators are asking each other for workarounds because they can't afford a WMS. The #2 result? NetSuite — an Oracle enterprise product. Your 5-person warehouse doesn't need Oracle. It needs a system that catches errors before the UPS truck leaves, for $0, by Friday.

The 30-second math that changes everything: Take your daily orders × your estimated error rate × $20/error × 250 shipping days. 300 orders/day at 3% = 9 errors/day = $180/day = $45,000/year. That's not a rounding error — that's payroll for another picker.

Why "we check at the pack station" is a lie

Every warehouse I visit says the same thing: "We check at the pack station." Then I watch the pack station for 20 minutes and see people taping boxes as fast as they can because the UPS truck leaves at 4:30. Nobody is checking anything. They're surviving. Here are the three failure points that make your pack station a rubber stamp:

  1. Similar-looking SKUs picked wrong. SKU ABC123 and ABC124 look identical on a shelf two aisles apart. Picker grabs the wrong one, pack station tapes it up — nobody compares the item to the order slip because the slip is buried under the box flap.
  2. Wrong quantity grabbed. Pick list says 2 units. Picker grabs 1. It's human — at 3pm on their 200th order, picker fatigue is real. Nobody counts items at pack station. They count boxes.
  3. Pack station confirms nothing. The pack station's job is tape, label, stack. Not verification. They're measured on throughput, not accuracy. You get what you measure — and you're measuring speed at the last checkpoint before the customer opens the box.

At hour 4 of a shift, error rates triple. Your 9am picker is sharp. Your 3pm picker is running on autopilot. And they're both using the same paper pick list with no safety net.

The $0 system, part 1: phone camera pick verification

Before a picker drops their bin at the pack station, they take one photo of the bin contents next to their pick list. That's it. 15 seconds. Here's why this one step catches 70% of errors:

  • Wrong item is visible. The photo shows the actual products. A wrong SKU is immediately obvious to anyone looking at the photo against the list — different color, different size, different label.
  • Wrong quantity is visible. Pick list says 3. Photo shows 2 items in the bin. Caught before the box gets taped.
  • Creates a timestamped record. Upload the photo via Google Form. Every pick has a time-stamped photo attached to the order number. When a customer says "you sent the wrong thing," you can pull the photo and see exactly what was in the bin before it left.
  • The social pressure alone cuts errors. Pickers know their bin is being photographed. They slow down for the two seconds it takes to double-check against the list. That's the whole point.

Setup: Create a Google Form with two fields — "Order Number" (short text) and "Pick Bin Photo" (file upload). Link it to a Google Sheet. The sheet is your pick verification log. Bookmark the form on every picker's phone. Zero dollars, 20 minutes to set up.

Part 2: the $30 weight check

The cheapest verification device in any warehouse is already on Amazon: a USB postal scale. $30. Here's what you do with it:

Create a Google Sheet with two columns: every SKU and its weight (in ounces, to one decimal). Add a second sheet for order lookups: enter an order number, list the SKUs and quantities in that order, and a formula calculates the expected total weight. When the pack station places the sealed box on the scale, the actual weight goes into a cell. If the difference between actual and expected is more than 10%, that cell turns red.

What the weight check catches that the photo misses:

  • Wrong quantity inside a sealed box (2 units weigh twice as much as 1 — the photo showed 2, but the packer grabbed from a different bin)
  • Wrong item of similar appearance but different weight (a heavier variant of the same product)
  • Missing item (box is lighter than expected — something got left on the pack station table)

This takes 5 seconds at pack station. Place box on scale, glance at the cell color. Green = ship. Red = open and check. Setup time: 15 minutes in Google Sheets plus 10 minutes to plug in the scale and weigh your top 50 SKUs.

Part 3: the picker accuracy leaderboard

This is the part that costs nothing and changes everything. You already have the data — the photo verification catches errors, the weight check catches errors. Track which picker made each error. Put it in a Google Sheet. Monday morning, it looks like this:

Carlos: 347 picks this week, 3 errors — 99.1% accuracy
Dave: 312 picks this week, 12 errors — 96.2% accuracy
Maria: 298 picks this week, 5 errors — 98.3% accuracy

That's it. No meeting. No lecture. Carlos wants to stay #1. Dave sees the number and wants to beat Carlos. You didn't manage anything — the number did the managing. Public accuracy tracking (not punishment, just visibility) is the single highest-ROI behavior change in any warehouse operation. Humans respond to visible metrics. Make the metric visible.

Part 4: the 5-minute end-of-day reconciliation

Before the UPS truck leaves, one person runs a 5-minute check:

  1. Orders marked "shipped" in your system today: ___
  2. Photo verifications logged today: ___
  3. Weight checks passed today: ___

If #1 doesn't match #2, a box shipped without a verification photo. If #2 doesn't match #3, a bin was photographed but the weight check never happened. Either way, you catch the process failure before the customer does — and you can still pull the box off the pallet if the UPS truck hasn't left yet.

This also catches the nightmare scenario: the wrong box went to the wrong customer. If order #1047 should weigh 3.8 lbs and the scale reads 7.2 lbs, you just caught someone else's order going into the wrong box. That's a $35 return + a lost customer — stopped for 5 seconds on a $30 scale.

Your inventory count is accurate but $8,000 of it is dead stock bleeding storage fees every month. Here's how to find it and what to do about it

You've got the accuracy system from part 1–4 running. Your pick/pack error rate is down. But there's a second inventory problem that accuracy alone doesn't fix: inventory that's perfectly accurate but completely dead. Correct count, zero demand, accumulating storage fees every day it sits.

A seller we worked with had 87 units of a product that sold great in 2024. In 2026, it's been sitting in FBA for 14 months. Amazon charges them $210/month in storage fees. The product cost $2,400 to manufacture. They've already paid $2,940 in storage. At this point, they're paying Amazon to store their own inventory — and they haven't made a move because "maybe it'll sell next month." That's $5,340 spent on a $2,400 product that hasn't sold a single unit this year. The inventory count is accurate. The financial decision is the problem.

The dead stock math that changes everything: Take every SKU that hasn't sold in 180+ days. Multiply unit cost × quantity on hand for total inventory value. Multiply monthly storage cost × units × months sitting. If storage cost exceeds 50% of inventory value, you've already paid Amazon more to store it than it's worth — and every month you wait makes it worse. Most sellers don't run this calculation because the storage fee shows up on a completely different report from the SKU-level sales data. The two numbers never meet. Until you make them meet.

The dead stock identification formula

Build one more tab in your inventory spreadsheet — the Aged Inventory Report. Here are the columns:

  • SKU and product name.
  • Units in stock. From your inventory management system or FBA Inventory report.
  • Unit cost. What you paid to manufacture or acquire each unit.
  • Total inventory value. Units × unit cost.
  • Days since last sale. Today minus the date of the last order containing this SKU.
  • Monthly storage cost per unit. For FBA: $0.78/cu ft (Jan–Sep) or $2.40+/cu ft (Oct–Dec) × cubic feet per unit ÷ number of units in that cubic foot. For your own warehouse: square footage cost allocation.
  • Total storage cost incurred. Monthly storage × months sitting.
  • Storage cost as % of item value. Total storage ÷ total inventory value × 100.
  • Flag: RED = storage cost > 50% of value (you're paying more to store than the product is worth — liquidate immediately). YELLOW = 25–50% (danger zone — decide this week). GREEN = under 25% (monitor, but not urgent yet).

This takes 30 minutes to set up and about 10 minutes to update monthly. The first time you run it, you'll probably find 2–5 SKUs in the red zone that you've been ignoring because "the margin is good" — margin that disappeared into storage fees six months ago.

The 5 dead stock action paths

Once you've identified your dead stock, you have exactly five options. Here they are, ranked from highest recovery to fastest resolution:

Path Recovery % Time to Execute Best For
1. Bundle with fast-mover 60–80% of cost 1–2 weeks Complementary products — dead SKU pairs naturally with a popular item. "Buy X, get Y free" or discounted bundle. Recovers most of your cost while moving the inventory.
2. Liquidate to a liquidator 10–20% of cost 2–4 weeks Branded products with resale value. Contact B-Stock, 888 Lots, or similar. You'll take a bath, but $480 now is better than $2,940 in storage fees over 14 months plus eventual disposal.
3. Deep-discount fire sale 30–50% of cost 1–3 weeks Products with some demand at the right price. Slash price 60–80%, run as a Lightning Deal or coupon. The goal is to clear inventory before storage fees eat another month.
4. Donate and write off Tax deduction: 25–37% of cost 1 week Products with zero resale value but deductible as charitable contribution. You get the tax write-off immediately and stop paying storage fees. Requires proper documentation.
5. Destroy / removal order 0% (loss is capped) 1–2 weeks Products with zero resale value and no donation channel. Amazon charges $0.97–$2.43/unit for disposal. It stings once, then it's over. Better than paying storage fees in perpetuity.

The decision framework in one sentence: If storage fees incurred > current liquidation value, you should have acted months ago — act today. Every day of indecision on a RED-flagged SKU costs you real money, and the math only gets worse. The best time to liquidate dead stock was when it hit 180 days without a sale. The second-best time is right now.

The FBA aged inventory survival guide

If you use Fulfillment by Amazon, dead stock has an extra dimension: Amazon's long-term storage fee schedule is designed to force you to act. Here's the 2026 timeline:

  • 0–180 days: Monthly inventory storage fee only (standard rates). No penalty for holding. This is your selling window.
  • 181–270 days: Aged inventory surcharge kicks in — roughly $1.50/cu ft on top of the monthly storage fee. For a standard-size product at 0.5 cu ft, that's an extra $0.75/unit/month.
  • 271–365 days: Surcharge increases to $3.80+/cu ft. Your storage costs are now 3–5× the standard rate. Most sellers hit this tier without realizing because the charge appears on a different report page from their daily sales.
  • 365+ days: $6.90+/cu ft surcharge. At this point, if your product sells for under $20, you're probably losing money every month just storing it. Removal order immediately.

How to run your Aged Inventory Report in Seller Central: Inventory → Inventory Planning → Inventory Age. This shows every FBA SKU broken into age buckets: 0–90 days, 91–180, 181–270, 271–365, and 365+. Export to CSV. Match against your SKU profitability tracker. Any SKU in the 271+ bucket: make a decision this week. The storage fees you're paying on that inventory are almost certainly exceeding any potential profit if it eventually sells.

Prevention: the reorder formula that stops dead stock before it starts

Dead stock isn't just a cleanup problem — it's a purchasing problem. Most dead inventory started as a purchase order that was sized wrong. Here's how to prevent it:

The MOQ trap: Your supplier's minimum order quantity is 500 units. You sell 8 per month. That's 62 months of inventory — more than 5 years. Even if every unit sells eventually, you've tied up capital and paid storage on product you won't need for years. The fix: negotiate a lower MOQ with a price premium, or find a supplier with smaller minimums. Paying 20% more per unit for an MOQ of 100 beats paying storage on 400 excess units for 5 years.

The reorder formula: Reorder Point = (Lead Time in Days × Daily Demand) + Safety Stock. Maximum Inventory = Reorder Point + Economic Order Quantity — but capped by: (1) shelf life, (2) storage cost per unit per month, (3) cost of capital. If storage cost exceeds 2% of unit cost per month, the EOQ shrinks dramatically — it's cheaper to reorder more frequently in smaller quantities than to pay storage on excess inventory. Your spreadsheet should calculate this automatically based on your actual sales velocity, not your supplier's preferred order size.

Across 250 shipping days, that's $45,000/year. A more realistic 5% error rate (paper pick lists, no verification) pushes it to $75,000/year. What should I do with inventory that hasn't sold in a year?

You have five options ranked by recovery rate: (1) Bundle it with a fast-moving product — "Buy X, get Y free" — recover 60–80% of cost. (2) Run a deep-discount fire sale at 60–80% off — recover 30–50%. (3) Liquidate to a liquidator like B-Stock or 888 Lots — recover 10–20% but it's fast. (4) Donate and write off — recover 25–37% through tax deduction, stops storage fees immediately. (5) Create a removal/disposal order — recover nothing, but the bleeding stops. The worst choice is doing nothing — every month you wait, the storage fees compound and the recovery percentage drops. For FBA sellers specifically: if an item is over 365 days old, the $6.90+/cu ft surcharge means you're almost certainly losing money every month you keep it. Removal order immediately, then decide which of the 5 paths to take once it's back in your possession.

How do I calculate whether it's cheaper to store or liquidate dead inventory?

The formula is: (Monthly storage cost × expected months until sale) vs (Unit cost − liquidation recovery). If the storage cost until you expect to sell exceeds what you'd lose by liquidating now, liquidate. Example: a product costs you $18/unit. Monthly storage is $3.50/unit. You think it might sell in 8 months. Storage cost: 8 × $3.50 = $28/unit — already more than the unit is worth. Liquidation at 20% recovery: you lose $14.40/unit but stop paying $3.50/month immediately. The liquidation is cheaper after just 4.1 months of storage. Most sellers miss this because the storage fee and the unit cost live in different mental buckets — the unit cost happened 18 months ago (sunk cost), but the storage fee is a new expense every month (active cost). The math doesn't care when you paid for the inventory — it only cares what you'll pay going forward versus what you can recover today.
