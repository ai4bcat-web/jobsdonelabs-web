Stop Losing Track of Estimates: A Free Job Tracking Spreadsheet for Small Contractors

By Ryne Bandolik · Jobs Done Labs · August 5, 2026

Key takeaways

Small contractors lose 15–25% of potential revenue to estimates they forgot to follow up on. For a $300K contractor, that's $45K–$75K/year in jobs that went to competitors who simply called back. The fix isn't expensive software — it's a free 3-tab spreadsheet with auto reminders and a 15-minute Monday ritual. Here's exactly how to build it, plus a downloadable template you can start using today.

The real cost of estimate amnesia

You've done it. Every contractor has. You send an estimate to a promising lead — nice kitchen remodel, full HVAC replacement, new deck — and they say "let me think about it." You make a mental note to follow up. Then Tuesday happens. And Wednesday. A water heater emergency in Westfield, a framing crew that ran short on lumber, a supplier who shipped the wrong tile.

Three weeks later you remember that estimate. You call. They already hired someone else. "Oh, we thought you were too busy," they say. What they mean is: your competitor followed up and you didn't.

This isn't a one-off. It's a pattern. Industry surveys on contractor forums and data from business coaches consistently show that small contractors lose 15–25% of their potential revenue to unfollowed estimates — not because the work was bad or the price was wrong, but because nobody circled back. For a contractor doing $300K in annual revenue with a 25–30% close rate, that's $11,250 to $18,750 in lost jobs per quarter. Over a year: $45,000 to $75,000.

The math is brutal but simple: You sent 120 estimates last year. You closed 30 of them (25% close rate). Of the 90 you didn't close, how many did you follow up with more than once? Twice? At all? If just 10 of those 90 — barely 11% — would have closed with a second follow-up, and your average job is $4,500, that's $45,000 you left on the table. Not because you're bad at what you do. Because you had no system for following up.

The Estimate & Job Tracker — template walkthrough

The spreadsheet has three tabs. That's it. Three tabs, some conditional formatting, a few auto-calculated fields, and you have a system that runs on Monday mornings in 15 minutes flat. Here's what goes in each tab:

Tab 1: Active Estimates

This is where every estimate lives until it's either won or lost. No more "which folder did I save that quote in?" or scrolling through your sent emails looking for the one you sent to the Smiths three weeks ago.

Columns: Estimate #, Client Name, Phone, Email, Job Type, Estimate Date, Amount, Follow-Up Date, Status (Won / Lost / Pending), Notes.

The Follow-Up Date column is the most important field in the entire spreadsheet. Set it to 5 business days after the estimate date. That's your trigger. Every Monday morning, you sort by this column, and every estimate past its follow-up date gets a call, text, or email — that day. No exceptions.

Conditional formatting tip: Make the Follow-Up Date cell turn red when today's date passes it. Then orange when it's within 2 days. Then green when it's more than 2 days out. One glance at the color tells you what needs attention.

Tab 2: Active Jobs

Once an estimate is won, it moves to Tab 2. This is your live dashboard of everything in progress, from the day the contract is signed to the day the final invoice is paid.

Columns: Job #, Client, Start Date, Crew Assigned, Materials Ordered? (Yes/No), Permit Status (Pending/Approved/N/A), Invoiced? (Yes/No), Paid? (Yes/No), Notes.

The genius of this tab is the Invoiced? and Paid? columns. How many jobs finish and the invoice doesn't go out for a week because you're busy? How many invoices go out and the payment doesn't come in because you forgot to follow up? These two Y/N columns make it impossible to lose track. Every Friday, sort by Invoiced=No for jobs that ended this week — and send those invoices. Then sort by Paid=No, Invoiced=Yes — and send payment reminders.

Tab 3: Weekly Dashboard

Auto-calculated counts that update from Tabs 1 and 2. This is what you look at on Monday morning while you drink your coffee:

  • Estimates sent this week: COUNTIF of estimate dates in the current week
  • Follow-ups due today: COUNTIF of follow-up dates ≤ today with status=Pending
  • Jobs in progress: COUNTIF of jobs with Paid=No and Start Date before today
  • Invoices outstanding: COUNTIF of Invoiced=Yes, Paid=No
  • Revenue won this month: SUMIF of amounts for status=Won in the current month

The dashboard takes 10 seconds to scan and tells you exactly what needs your attention: "5 follow-ups overdue, 3 invoices unpaid past 30 days, 8 jobs in progress." Suddenly you're not guessing — you know.

The 15-minute Monday ritual

Every Monday morning, before you check email, before you call the crew, before you do anything else — you do this:

  1. Open Tab 3 — Dashboard. Glance at the numbers. How many follow-ups are due? How many invoices are outstanding? This frames your priorities for the week.
  2. Open Tab 1 — Active Estimates. Sort by Follow-Up Date. Every estimate past its follow-up date gets action: a text ("Hey, just checking in — any questions on that deck estimate?"), a call, or an email. Update the Follow-Up Date to +7 days and add a note ("texted 8/5, said still deciding").
  3. Open Tab 2 — Active Jobs. Quick scan: Any jobs that finished this week with Invoiced=No? Send those invoices now. Any invoices over 30 days with Paid=No? Send a payment reminder.

Total time: 15 minutes. Results: 2–3 recovered jobs per month for most contractors who stick with it. The ritual works because it's mechanical — you don't decide to follow up, the spreadsheet tells you to follow up. That's the difference between a system and a mental note.

What one contractor told us: "I started tracking estimates in a spreadsheet three months ago. First month I recovered a $7,200 bathroom remodel I'd completely forgotten about. The customer said 'I was waiting for you to call back.' That one job paid for the 45 minutes it took to build the spreadsheet — 160 times over."

One follow-up isn't enough. Here's the 3-touch cadence that closes 45% instead of 25%.

The Monday ritual is your floor, not your ceiling. A single follow-up recovers the easy wins — the customer who was quietly waiting for you to call back. But most lost estimates aren't waiting. They're deciding, and deciding takes time. One check-in isn't a system; it's a coin flip.

Here's the conversion math that changes everything. Contractors who send an estimate and follow up zero times close about 25% of the people they quote. Contractors who run a three-touch cadence — a light check-in on day 3, a value-add on day 7, and a close-out on day 14 — close 40-50% of the same pool. A 20-point lift on 12 estimates a month at a $4,500 average job is $10,800 a month in additional closed revenue. The cost? Three two-minute texts per estimate.

The cadence, step by step:

  • Day 0 — set the expectation (when you send the estimate). "I'll follow up Wednesday if I don't hear from you." No pressure, just a heads-up. Now the follow-up isn't an interruption — it's something you promised.
  • Day 3 — the checking-in text. "Hey, just checking if you had any questions on that deck estimate. Happy to walk through anything." Two sentences, personable, zero hard sell. This one touch recovers most of the easy wins.
  • Day 7 — the value-add. Send a before/after photo or a case study from a similar project. Give first, ask later. This is the touch that separates you from the contractor who just texted "so, you in?"
  • Day 14 — the close-out. "I'm closing this estimate out Friday. Here's a link to grab 10 minutes if you want to go over it." Urgency without pressure. Most of the 40-50% closes happen on this touch — because now the customer has seen you show up three times, and showing up is rare.

The 3 texts, ready to copy and send

  1. Day 3: "Hey [name], just checking if you had any questions on the [project] estimate I sent. Happy to walk through anything over the phone."
  2. Day 7: "[Name], here's a before/after from a [similar project] we just wrapped. Thought it might help you picture [the result]."
  3. Day 14: "[Name], I'm closing this estimate out Friday. If you'd like 10 minutes to go over it, here's my calendar: [link]. If not, no worries — you know where to find me."

Add four columns to Tab 1 so the cadence runs itself: Follow-Up 1 Date + Method, Follow-Up 2 Date + Method, Final Follow-Up Date, and Win/Loss Outcome. The outcome column is the one that matters most — it auto-calculates your follow-up conversion rate, so you can watch 25% become 45% in real time instead of guessing.

Why most contractors only follow up once: they treat the follow-up as a chore to survive, not a sequence to run — and they're afraid of being annoying. But a customer who asked you to quote them isn't annoyed by a follow-up; they're annoyed by being ghosted by three contractors who promised to call. The three-touch cadence isn't pestering. It's the difference between "the guy who quoted me" and "the guy who actually wanted the job." If you're worried you're burning follow-ups on tire-kickers, run them through the tire-kicker filter first. The key insight: the process matters more than the tool. A contractor with a disciplined Monday ritual and a basic spreadsheet will outperform a contractor with a $300/month software subscription they use haphazardly. Master the process on a spreadsheet. Then, when you're ready, you'll know exactly what you need software to do — and you won't overpay for features you'll never touch.

Frequently asked questions

I already use QuickBooks — do I still need a job tracking spreadsheet?

Yes, and here's why: QuickBooks tracks money. It tells you what was invoiced, what was paid, and what's outstanding. It doesn't tell you which estimates need follow-up, which jobs are waiting on permits, or which crew is assigned where. QuickBooks is your accounting system. The spreadsheet is your operations system. They serve different purposes, and trying to make QuickBooks do operations is like using a checkbook as a project plan. Use both — the spreadsheet runs the field; QuickBooks runs the books.

What about lead tracking — should estimates and new leads be in the same spreadsheet?

Keep them separate, at least at first. Leads are unqualified inquiries — people who filled out a form or called asking "how much for a bathroom?" Estimates are qualified opportunities where you've already had a conversation, confirmed budget, and sent a real price. Mixing them in one spreadsheet creates noise: 30 leads where 25 never became estimates cluttering up your view of the 5 estimates that actually need follow-up. Once a lead becomes an estimate (you've seen the job and sent a number), it graduates to Tab 1. Before that, it lives in a simple inquiry log. We cover the inquiry-to-estimate pipeline in our lead response guide.

How do I get my crew to use the spreadsheet?

You don't — and that's by design. The spreadsheet is for the owner or office manager. The crew doesn't need to see estimates, follow-up dates, or invoice status. They need to know where to show up and what to do — that's a separate daily dispatch sheet (a printed one-pager from Tab 2 with today's jobs, addresses, and notes). Trying to get field crews to update a spreadsheet in real time is a recipe for frustration, for them and for you. The spreadsheet is your command center. The crew gets paper dispatches and texts.

How do I follow up on an estimate without being annoying?

Lead with help, not a sales pitch. A follow-up that asks a question — "any questions on that deck estimate?" — is welcome; one that just says "so, you in?" feels like pressure. Three rules make it comfortable. First, set the expectation at day 0: tell them you'll follow up, so when it lands it's a promise kept, not an interruption. Second, give before you ask — the day-7 touch should be a before/after photo or case study, not another "just checking in." Third, cap it at three touches over two weeks, then let go gracefully. Customers who asked you to quote them aren't annoyed by a follow-up; they're annoyed by being ghosted by contractors who promised to call and never did. The annoying thing isn't the follow-up — it's the hard sell.

How many times should I follow up on a quote before giving up?

Three touches over two weeks, then stop: day 3 (the checking-in text), day 7 (the value-add), day 14 (the close-out). That's the sweet spot — enough touches to move the decision, few enough that it never feels like pestering. Contractor-forum and business-coach data is consistent on the curve: win rates climb from roughly 25% with zero follow-ups to 40-50% with three, then flatten. A fourth or fifth touch adds almost nothing and starts to annoy. After the day-14 close-out, mark the estimate lost in your spreadsheet — and if it's the right kind of lead, drop them into a 60-day nurture (a quarterly check-in) instead of continuing to chase.

What's the best estimate follow-up text template for contractors?

Keep it to two sentences, personable, and specific to their project. The best-performing pattern we've seen is the day-3 check-in: "Hey [name], just checking if you had any questions on the [project] estimate I sent. Happy to walk through anything." It works because it asks a question instead of demanding a decision, and it references their actual job rather than a generic "checking in." For the day-7 touch, send a before/after photo or a case study from a similar project — give first, ask later. For day 14, add gentle urgency: "I'm closing this estimate out Friday. If you'd like 10 minutes to go over it, here's my calendar." The full three-text sequence, ready to copy, is in the cadence section above.
