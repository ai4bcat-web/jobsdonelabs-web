"The Investigator Asked For Batch Record #147 And Maria Spent 76 Minutes Finding It"

You run a 15-person contract manufacturer making dietary supplements, protein powders, and functional beverages. You produce 20 batches per month — 240 per year. At 10 pages per batch record, that's 2,400 pages per year going into filing cabinets. Your QA manager (who also handles customer service and shipping) maintains a separate Excel index that cross-references batch numbers to products and filing cabinet drawers — except the index hasn't been updated in 6 months because she's been too busy. The last 120 batch records are in a "to be filed" pile on her desk.

Then the FDA investigator arrives for a routine GMP inspection. On Day 2, she says: "I'd like to see the batch production record for lot #SD-147, product SuperGreens Powder, manufactured approximately 18 months ago. And the raw material receiving records for the organic spirulina lot used in that batch, plus the finished product certificate of analysis from your third-party lab."

Batch #SD-147 is in the unfiled pile. Maria spends 45 minutes digging — finds it at 11:32 AM. The raw material record is in a DIFFERENT filing cabinet. She finds that at 11:48 AM. The third-party lab COA is in her EMAIL — she's been receiving them as PDFs for 4 months without printing and filing them. She downloads it from Gmail and prints it at 12:03 PM. Total retrieval time from request: 76 minutes.

The FDA investigator's notes: "Batch record retrieval for lot SD-147 took 76 minutes. Raw material receiving record retrieval required cross-referencing across two filing systems. Finished product COA not maintained in batch record file — retrieved from email archive. Recordkeeping system does not ensure timely retrieval of all records associated with a batch."

That observation becomes FDA Form 483 Observation #3. It doesn't become a Warning Letter — this time. But it goes into your permanent FDA compliance history. Next inspection, if records are still disorganized, it escalates. And your private-label clients? They see the 483 on FDA's public database and demand their own audits.

What The FDA Actually Requires In A Batch Production Record — 21 CFR Part 111.83 And 117.315 In Plain English

Here's every field the FDA requires, translated from regulatory language into what you actually need to document:

  1. Product identity: Product name, batch/lot number, and date of manufacture. The lot number must be unique and traceable through your entire system.
  2. Complete ingredient list: Every ingredient with its supplier lot number, quantity weighed, and the person who weighed it. This is the foundation of lot traceability — if a raw material lot is later found contaminated, this is how you identify every finished batch that used it.
  3. Equipment used: Which blender, filler, capper, and labeler were used — with verification that each was properly cleaned and sanitized before use. Document the cleaning verification.
  4. In-process checks: What was checked (blend time, fill weights, cap torque, label count, metal detector), when it was checked (every 30 minutes is standard), who checked it, the pass/fail result, and corrective action if it failed.
  5. Finished product testing: Results from microbial testing, heavy metals, potency/assay — either from your in-house lab or third-party lab. Include the lab's certificate of analysis.
  6. Packaging and labeling verification: Confirm the correct label was applied, the lot code and expiration date are correct and legible, and the net weight is within tolerance.
  7. Yield reconciliation: Theoretical yield (what should have been produced based on ingredient quantities) vs actual yield (what was actually packaged). Investigate and explain any variance greater than 2%.
  8. QA review and release: Signature and date of the QA person who reviewed the complete batch record and approved the batch for release. This is the final gate — no product ships without it.

21 CFR Retention Requirements At A Glance

  • Dietary supplements (Part 111): Retain batch records for 2 years past the expiration date of the batch
  • Food (Part 117): Retain for 2 years from date of manufacture
  • Private-label agreements: Some customers require 5+ years — check your contracts
  • Retrieval requirement: All records associated with a batch must be retrievable within 24 hours of an FDA request — not 76 minutes of panic-searching through piles

How To Transition From Paper To Electronic In One Weekend

This isn't a 4-8 week MES implementation. It's a structured weekend of focused work. Here's the step-by-step plan:

Friday (3 hours)

  • 4:00–5:30 PM: Download the batch record template. Customize the dropdown menus with your actual products, equipment list, and standard in-process checks. Enter your ingredient library so weighing operators can select from a list instead of handwriting ingredient names.
  • 5:30–7:00 PM: Set up the lot traceability matrix structure. Create the batch record index with columns for all searchable fields. The matrix will be populated with data tomorrow — tonight is about structure.

Saturday (5 hours)

  • 9:00 AM–12:00 PM: Enter all raw material receiving records from the last 12 months into the lot traceability matrix. Pull supplier COAs from your filing cabinet (or email). Cross-reference each raw material lot with the finished product batches it was used in.
  • 1:00–3:00 PM: Build the batch record index. Enter every batch from the last 12 months: batch number, product, date, customer, raw material lots used, and current status. This is the most time-intensive step — but once it's done, retrieval is instant forever.

Sunday (4 hours)

  • 9:00–11:00 AM: Scan the last 12 months of paper batch records. Use a phone scanning app like Adobe Scan (free) — the FDA accepts legible digital copies. Save as PDFs in a clearly named folder structure: /BatchRecords/YYYY-MM/BatchNumber.pdf.
  • 11:00 AM–1:00 PM: Create the FDA inspection binder. Pre-print and organize these documents in a single 3-ring binder with labeled tabs: organizational chart and quality policy, last 12 months of batch records (tabbed by batch number), raw material receiving records with supplier COAs, finished product COAs from third-party lab, equipment cleaning and maintenance logs, employee GMP training records, pest control records, water testing records (if applicable), customer complaint log, and recall plan.
The Sunday afternoon test: At 1:00 PM, have someone (not Maria) pick a random batch number from 8 months ago. Time how long it takes Maria to produce: (a) the complete batch record, (b) the raw material receiving records for every ingredient lot in that batch, (c) the finished product COA, and (d) the equipment cleaning log for the equipment used. Target: under 5 minutes total. If you hit it, you're inspection-ready.

The FDA Inspection Readiness Checklist: What To Have Pre-Printed And Organized Before The Investigator Walks Through The Door

FDA GMP inspections are usually unannounced — but you know they're coming eventually if you're registered as a food facility (every 2-3 years is typical for low-risk facilities). Here's what should be in your inspection binder at all times, not assembled in a panic the morning of:

The Inspection-Ready Binder: 10 Tabs

  1. Organizational chart and quality policy — shows the FDA who's responsible for what
  2. Last 12 months of batch production records — tabbed by batch/lot number, with raw material COAs, in-process checks, finished product COAs, and QA release signatures all collated together for each batch
  3. Raw material receiving records with supplier COAs — organized by supplier, then by lot number
  4. Finished product COAs from third-party lab — organized by batch number
  5. Equipment cleaning and maintenance logs — showing cleaning verification and preventive maintenance schedule
  6. Employee GMP training records — all employees must have documented training within 90 days of hire and annually thereafter, per 21 CFR 111.8
  7. Pest control records — from your pest control service, documenting regular inspections and any corrective actions
  8. Water testing records — if water is used as an ingredient, you need annual potability testing plus any additional testing your product requires
  9. Customer complaint log and recall plan — documented complaints with investigation results, and a written recall plan that's been tested (mock recall) within the last year
  10. Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) — for receiving, storage, production, sanitation, labeling, and shipping

How To Handle The FDA Investigator: What To Say, What NOT To Say

The single most important rule of FDA inspections: answer only the question asked, and nothing more. Don't volunteer information. Don't speculate. Don't say "I think" or "probably" or "usually." If you don't know the answer, say "I'll need to check our records and get back to you" — and then actually follow up. An FDA investigator's primary goal is to determine whether your facility is operating in a state of control. Disorganized records create the impression that your production processes might also be disorganized. A 483 observation about recordkeeping is often the gateway to deeper scrutiny — because if you can't find your batch records, what else can't you find?
