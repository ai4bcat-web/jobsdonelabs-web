What a lost renewal actually costs

A maintenance plan customer isn't just a $299-499/year line on a spreadsheet. They're the customer who calls you for every repair, recommends you to two neighbors, and stays 3-4 years. Conservatively, one plan customer is worth $1,500-2,000 over their lifetime — not counting the $400-800 repair calls they make between tune-ups.

Now do the math on what you lose when a renewal slips.

The cost of one forgotten renewal
  • Average plan value: $400/year × 3.5 years retention = $1,400 direct plan revenue lost
  • 1-2 referrals over the relationship (at 0.5 referrals × $400 average): $200-400 in referral revenue lost
  • 3-4 repair calls over the relationship that now go to a competitor ($400 avg each): $1,200-1,600 in repair revenue lost
  • Total: one lost renewal = roughly $2,800-3,400 in lifetime revenue handed to a competitor

Losing just one renewal per month — which is what happens when the "system" is a manila folder or an office manager who also does payroll, AP, and phones — is $33,000-40,000 per year in invisible churn. It doesn't show on any P&L because it's revenue that should have renewed, not revenue that was on the books and walked.

Now contrast: a 50-plan book at 85% renewal is $17,000/year in plan revenue. The same book at 98% renewal — achievable with a tracking spreadsheet and a no-pressure 4-touch sequence — is roughly $19,600/year. That $2,600 difference came from a spreadsheet, not from a sales call or a marketing campaign. And the 50th customer who stayed instead of calling a competitor is the one who refers your shop to the whole neighborhood.

The free maintenance plan renewal tracker spreadsheet

Everything below is built into a single Google Sheets workbook you can copy, rename for your shop, and start using in under an hour. No macros, no add-ons, no monthly fee.

Dashboard tab — at-a-glance renewal health

  • Total active plans (auto-counted from the customer tab)
  • Plans renewing this month, next month, and this quarter
  • Renewal revenue projected by quarter (plan value × count of plans renewing that quarter)
  • Renewal rate % (auto-calculated: RENEWED ÷ total expired or renewed)
  • Plans past due — count and total dollar value of un-renewed plans now in the red
  • Year-over-year comparison: this quarter renewal revenue vs. same quarter last year

Per-customer tracker tab — every plan, one row

  • Customer name, address, phone, and email
  • Equipment type and age (furnace, AC, heat pump — age matters for repair-to-plan conversion, covered below)
  • Plan type: Annual, Semi-Annual, or Quarterly
  • Plan start date and renewal date
  • Number of visits included per year (1 or 2)
  • Date of last maintenance visit
  • Next scheduled visit date
  • Plan value ($/year)
  • Payment method (credit card on file, check, invoiced)
  • Auto-renew? (Y/N)
  • Renewal status column — RENEWED / PENDING <30d / PENDING <60d / PAST DUE / CANCELLED

Color-coded alerts: Conditional formatting auto-highlights: YELLOW when renewal is within 60 days, AMBER within 30 days, and RED when past due. You open the sheet on Monday morning and the color-coding tells you who needs a call this week — no digging, no remembering.

Renewal communication log tab

Tracks which touch each customer received: Email at 60 days (✓), Email at 30 days (✓), Phone call at 14 days (✓), Final notice at 7 days (✓), plus any customer response notes. No more wondering "did we call the Johnsons about their spring tune-up?" — the log has the timestamp.

Warranty-claim log tab

Added to the same workbook: while the tech is there for the tune-up, any failing part still under manufacturer warranty becomes a recoverable claim. Track the date, customer, equipment, part, manufacturer, warranty status, claim filed date, claim reference number, documentation submitted, and reimbursement received date and amount. This tab turns a maintenance visit that finds a covered failure from a paperwork nightmare into a revenue recovery event — covered in detail below. If your tech finds a $300 blower motor that Carrier's warranty covers, and your office forgets to file the claim within the 60-day window, you ate the $300. The log prevents that.

The off-season scheduling strategy that fills spring and fall

Here's the scheduling rhythm that separates shops running at 98% renewal from the ones bleeding renewals into the "call us when you're ready" void:

Off-season cadence

  • Spring tune-ups (March-May): Book them in January. When competitors are slow and your proactive callback to an existing plan customer sounds like above-and-beyond service.
  • Fall tune-ups (September-October): Book them in July. Same logic — you're calling when nobody else is, and you're confirming a calendar date, not asking if they want to renew.
  • 60-day advance scheduling: Operators who pre-schedule report show rates around 85%, versus roughly 45% for "we'll call you when it's time." The calendar appointment is the commitment. The tracker auto-calculates next-visit dates from the last-visit date, so you always know who's due when.
  • No-show buffer: Schedule 5-10% more visits than you have capacity, knowing that pre-scheduled 60-day-out appointments will have some reschedules. The tracker's scheduling queue tab shows who's confirmed and who hasn't responded.

Converting repair calls into maintenance plans

The highest-converting moment for a maintenance plan isn't a Facebook ad or a postcard. It's the repair visit, after the AC is blowing cold air and the customer is relieved. The tech hands them a card — not a pitch, not a brochure, just the math:

"Your system is running great. Our $299 annual plan covers two tune-ups, priority scheduling, and if anything fails between visits you skip the dispatch fee. No pressure — just letting you know it exists."

At a 15% conversion rate — the low end of what operators report — here's how the math works for a 5-tech shop:

  • 5 techs × 8 calls/day × 250 working days = 10,000 service calls per year
  • 15% conversion = 1,500 new plan customers per year
  • At $299-499/plan = $450,000-750,000 in new recurring revenue
  • Cost to acquire: the price of a business card. Zero marketing spend.

The tracker's conversion-tracking tab logs: which tech made the offer, what the repair was, whether the customer accepted, and the plan start date. After 90 days you know which techs convert at 25% and which haven't handed out a single card — and you can coach accordingly.

The renewal email sequence that gets 85%+ re-enrollment without a single sales call

Four touches, zero upsells. The goal isn't to sell them something new — it's to not lose what they already bought.

4-touch renewal sequence

  1. 60 days before renewal — Email: "Your ComfortGuard plan renewes on [date]. Everything stays the same — same price, same 2 tune-ups, same priority scheduling. Reply YES to lock it in." No pricing change, no new terms, just confirmation.
  2. 30 days before renewal — Email: "Quick reminder — your plan renewes next month. Your spring tune-up is already on the schedule for [date]. No action needed — just confirming." The scheduled appointment is the retention anchor.
  3. 14 days before renewal — Phone call: Two minutes, personable. "Just making sure everything's good with the system since the last tune-up. While I have you — your plan renewes on the 15th, everything stays the same. We good?" This call is the highest-converting single touch because it's a human voice.
  4. 7 days before renewal — Final notice: "Your ComfortGuard plan auto-renews on [date] at the current rate unless we hear otherwise. Reply STOP to cancel." Low-friction, no guilt. They stay because they want to.

The templates are in the spreadsheet's communication log tab, ready to copy-paste into your email client or CRM. Adjust the bracketed fields ([date], plan name, next visit date) per customer — takes 20 seconds per renewal.

While you're there for the tune-up — the warranty claim that pays for the visit

Here's a scenario that plays out hundreds of times a day in HVAC shops across the country: a tech is doing a routine maintenance visit and finds a failing blower motor. It's 4 years old. The manufacturer's warranty is 5 years, parts and labor. The tech replaces the motor, files a quick service report, and moves on. Six weeks later, Carrier denies the claim — missing a field on the report. The shop eats an $800 part and 2 hours of tech time. The maintenance visit that should have been profitable just went negative.

The warranty-claim log tab in the renewal tracker prevents this. For every claim:

  • Date of visit and customer name
  • Equipment, make, model, serial number
  • Part replaced and part number
  • Manufacturer and warranty coverage window (5 years, 10 years, etc.)
  • Warranty status: COVERED / DENIED / PENDING / EXPIRED
  • Claim filed date
  • Claim reference number from the manufacturer's portal
  • Documentation submitted checklist: service report with ambient temperature at time of failure, serial number photo, proof of purchase, install date
  • Reimbursement received date and dollar amount

The 60-day filing window is the trap. HVAC manufacturers (Carrier, Trane, Lennox, Rheem) all have 30-90 day claim windows from the date the part was removed, and the documentation requirements vary by manufacturer. Missing one field — ambient temperature at failure, which sounds like trivia — is the #1 reason claims are denied. The log's checklist column ensures every claim goes out with complete documentation. For a shop replacing 2-3 warranty-covered parts a month at an average of $400 each, that's $9,600-14,400/year in recoverable parts and labor that's currently written off as "cost of doing business."

The maintenance visit now has two revenue sources: the plan renewal (recurring) and the warranty recovery (one-time, from the same truck roll). The tracker shows both in the dashboard.
