How to Never Lose a Review When Your Tech Is Late

By Ryne Bandolik · Jobs Done Labs · October 11, 2026

Key takeaways

One bad review from an uncommunicated delay costs 10 to 15 lost leads per month — roughly eleven thousand to sixteen thousand dollars a year. The fix is not perfect scheduling (that is impossible). The fix is a six-stage SMS template system that turns every late tech into a customer who feels informed, respected, and still leaves five stars. Below: the exact templates, the delay-handling flowchart, the review-timing guide, and the ROI math that makes the thirty-minute setup worth it.

The one-star review that cost you 15 leads this month

A customer takes the morning off work to wait for your tech. The two-hour arrival window comes and goes. At 11:15am, 45 minutes past the window, the customer calls your office. Nobody answers — the dispatcher is on another line. At 11:45am the customer leaves a one-star review: "Took the morning off work. Nobody showed up. Nobody called. Unprofessional."

Here is what actually happened: the tech was on the previous job, which ran 90 minutes long because the customer added two extra repairs. Your dispatcher knew this at 9:30am — 105 minutes before the review was posted. Nobody texted the waiting customer. The tech arrived at 12:20pm, did excellent work, and the customer paid the invoice without complaint. But the one-star review is permanent.

The math on that one review: ninety-three percent of customers read reviews before calling a service business. Companies rated below 4.0 stars lose 40 to 60 percent of clicks to higher-rated competitors. If your average job is 300 dollars and you close30 percent of leads, one bad review costs roughly 1,350 dollars per month in lost revenue. Over a year: 16,200 dollars. From one review. One text could have prevented it.

Why "my techs are never late" is not the solution: Forty percent of service calls run late. The jobs before yours ran long, traffic was bad, the parts house was backed up. Late arrivals are an operations problem. One-star reviews from late arrivals are a communication problem. Fix the communication, and late arrivals become "they kept me updated the whole time — really professional" instead of "nobody called."

The free customer communication template pack: 6 stages, exact words

Every service call has six natural communication moments. Send the right message at each one, and the customer feels informed and in control — even when things go wrong. Copy and paste these into a shared Google Doc your dispatcher keeps open all day. Each template takes 15 seconds to personalize.

Stage 1: Day-before confirmation (send between 4pm and 6pm the day before)

Hi [Name], this is [Company] confirming your [service type] appointment tomorrow, [date]. Your tech [Tech name] will arrive between [window start] and [window end]. You will get a text when they are on the way. Reply STOP to opt out.

Why this works: Day-before confirmation cuts no-shows by 30 to 50 percent. It also gives the customer your number so tomorrow's texts are not from an unknown sender. For customers who booked more than a week ago, this is the difference between "oh right, the plumber is coming" and "who is this person at my door?"

Stage 2: On the way (send when tech leaves previous job)

Hi [Name], [Tech name] is on the way to your [service type] appointment. ETA: [time] based on current traffic. See you soon!

Why this works: This is the most important text in the sequence. It converts an abstract "between 8am and 10am" into a concrete "Chris is 18 minutes away." The customer can wrap up what they are doing, put the dog away, and unlock the gate. It also functions as a second confirmation — if the customer forgot about the appointment, this is their reminder with enough time to be ready.

Stage 3: Running late (send immediately when delay exceeds 15 minutes)

Hi [Name], quick update — [Tech name] is running about [X] minutes behind schedule. The previous job took longer than expected. Their new ETA is [new time]. We will text again when they are 10 minutes away. Thank you for your patience — we will make sure you are taken care of today.

Why this works: This is the template that prevents the one-star review. Three things happen in the customer's mind when they get this text: (1) "Okay, someone knows I exist," (2) "I have a new time so I can plan," (3) "They said 'we will make sure you are taken care of' — I trust them." The apology is embedded without being groveling. The commitment ("we will make sure") re-establishes reliability.

Stage 4: Ten minutes out (send when tech is 10 min away)

Hi [Name], [Tech name] is 10 minutes away. See you soon!

Why this works: The final heads-up. For customers who have been waiting (especially after a delay), this is the signal that the waiting is over. The tone is upbeat and brief — do not re-apologize, do not re-explain the delay. The delay is in the past. The tech is almost there.

Stage 5: Job complete (send within 30 minutes of job finishing)

Hi [Name], we just finished your [service type]. [Tech name] says [one-line summary of what was done, any findings, any next steps]. Your invoice will arrive by email shortly. If you have any questions, reply to this text or call us at [phone]. Thank you for choosing [Company]!

Why this works: This closes the loop. The customer knows what was done and what to expect next. The "reply to this text" line keeps the conversation channel open for questions. The one-line summary from the tech is the most valuable part — it proves the tech actually communicated with the office about the job, which builds trust for the next step.

Stage 6: Review request (send 2 to 3 hours after job complete, same day)

Hi [Name], glad we could help with your [service type] today. If you have a moment, a quick review helps other homeowners find us: [review link]. It means a lot to our team. Thank you!

Why this works: The timing is critical. Send immediately after job completion, and the customer feels pressured. Send the next day, and the goodwill has faded. Two to three hours post-completion is the sweet spot: the customer has had time to experience the result (the AC is cold, the leak is fixed) but the positive feeling is still fresh. The language is gratitude-based ("it means a lot to our team"), not transactional.

Do not send a review request if: (a) the job had unresolved issues, (b) the tech was more than 2 hours late without a clear resolution, (c) the customer expressed dissatisfaction during the job, or (d) the customer did not answer the door or interact with the tech. A review request on a bad experience amplifies the negative. Skip it, flag the job for a manager follow-up call instead, and earn the review after you have fixed the problem.

The delay handling decision flowchart

When a tech runs late, the dispatcher has about 60 seconds to decide what to communicate and to whom. The flowchart below makes it a binary decision, not a judgment call:

Delay communication flowchart

  1. Tech reports delay. Dispatcher confirms: (a) how many minutes behind? (b) what caused it? (c) can the rest of the day's jobs be adjusted to absorb this delay without cascading?
  2. Delay under 30 minutes: Send Stage 3 (Running Late) text to the next customer. Adjust the Stage 2 (On the Way) trigger to fire when the tech is actually leaving. No other action needed.
  3. Delay 30 to 90 minutes: Send Stage 3 text to the next customer. Call the customer after the text if the delay will push the appointment past the original window end time. Offer: (a) wait with updated ETA, (b) reschedule to a same-day later slot if available, or (c) reschedule to tomorrow with priority placement. Log what the customer chose.
  4. Delay over 90 minutes: Same as above, plus: offer a discount (10 to15 percent or a flat 25 to 50 dollars off) proactively in the phone call. "Mr. Smith, Chris is running about two hours behind and I want to be upfront with you — that is not the experience we aim for. We would like to take 15 percent off today's service as a thank-you for your flexibility. Does the new 3:30pm time still work, or should we reschedule?" The offer costs 30 to 75 dollars and saves a review that costs 1,350 dollars a month.
  5. Customer cannot be reached (text and call, no response after 15 min): Proceed with tech dispatch. The communication attempt is documented. If the customer leaves a negative review later, the record of attempted contact is your response: "We texted at 10:22am and called at 10:30am to let you know — we are sorry we could not reach you." This is far stronger than silence.

Frequently asked questions

What should I text a customer when my technician is running late?

Send a text as soon as you know the tech will be more than15 minutes behind. Include the tech's name, the new ETA, a brief honest reason, and what you are doing about it. Template: "Hi [Name], [Tech name] is running about [X] minutes behind — the previous job took longer than expected. New ETA: [time]. We will text again when they are10 minutes away. Thank you for your patience." The key is proactivity. A customer who gets an unprompted update is far less likely to leave a negative review than one who waits in silence.

How do I ask customers for reviews without sounding pushy?

Time the request to when satisfaction is highest:2 to3 hours after job completion, when the customer has experienced the result but the positive feeling is still fresh. Keep it short and gratitude-based: "Hi [Name], glad we could help today. If you have a moment, a quick review helps other homeowners find us: [link]. It means a lot to our team." Never ask for a review if the job had issues. Skip it, have a manager call, and earn the review after you have fixed the problem.

How much does a single bad review cost my service business?

Roughly 1,350 dollars per month in lost revenue, or 16,200 dollars per year. This is based on: one bad review causes 10 to15 lost leads per month (93 percent of customers read reviews, and businesses below 4.0 stars lose 40 to 60 percent of clicks), with an average job value of 300 dollars and a 30 percent lead close rate. Your specific cost will vary based on your average ticket and close rate, but the bottom line is constant: one bad review costs significantly more than any single job earns.

Should I use SMS or email to communicate with service customers?

SMS. Open rates are 98 percent for text messages versus 20 percent for email. Average response time is 90 seconds versus90 minutes. For time-sensitive service updates (running late, on the way, job complete), SMS is the only channel that reaches the customer in time to matter. Use email for invoices, receipts, service agreements, and post-job surveys. The templates above are designed for SMS; adapt them for email by adding your logo and a slightly more formal tone.

What if my techs are never late — do I still need these templates?

Yes — for two reasons. First, every shop has late arrivals sometimes, and the first time it happens without a communication system is the day you earn a permanent one-star review. Having the templates ready means your dispatcher does not have to think about what to say under pressure — they copy, paste, personalize, and send in 15 seconds. Second, the on-the-way, job-complete, and review-request templates improve the customer experience even when everything runs perfectly. Customers who receive proactive updates rate their experience higher than customers who receive great service in silence. Communication is not just damage control — it is a competitive advantage.
