What OCR actually asks for

In every HIPAA investigation, the OCR data request letter asks for five categories of documentation: (1) patient authorization and disclosure records, (2) breach incident logs and risk assessments, (3) business associate agreements for all vendors handling PHI, (4) staff HIPAA training records with attestation, and (5) the most recent security risk assessment with remediation plan. If you cannot produce all five within the thirty-day response window, the investigator does not need to find a violation — the absence of documentation IS the violation.

Tab 1 — Patient Authorization Log

Every disclosure of protected health information that is not for treatment, payment, or healthcare operations requires patient authorization — and the authorization must be documented, even if the disclosure is permitted. This tab tracks: patient name, date of disclosure, what was disclosed, to whom, purpose of disclosure, authorization expiration date, and revocation status. OCR investigators will ask for this log first — it is the most common documentation gap in small practices. A practice that discloses PHI to attorneys, employers, life insurers, or schools without logging those disclosures is documenting its own violations.

Tab 2 — Breach Incident Log With Five-Factor Risk Assessment

Every impermissible access, use, or disclosure of PHI must be logged and run through the five-factor risk assessment required by the Breach Notification Rule. This tab captures: date discovered, description of incident, number of individuals potentially affected, and the five-factor assessment: (a) nature and extent of PHI involved, (b) who received or accessed the PHI, (c) whether PHI was actually acquired or viewed, (d) extent of mitigation, and (e) the persons involved. The spreadsheet auto-scores low, medium, or high probability of compromise based on your inputs. Low probability means notification is not required — but the assessment itself must be documented. Medium or high probability triggers the notification cascade: affected individuals within sixty days, HHS/OCR within sixty days (or annually for breaches of fewer than five hundred), and media notification for breaches of five hundred or more. The tab also tracks notification status, OCR report filing date, and corrective action taken.

Tab 3 — Business Associate Agreement Tracker

A typical small medical practice works with eight to twelve vendors that handle PHI: EHR vendor, billing service, shredding company, IT support, cloud backup, email provider, patient portal, answering service, and potentially a collection agency, transcription service, and document storage company. Each one requires a business associate agreement — and missing even one BAA is the number one finding in every OCR investigation. This tab tracks: vendor name, service provided, agreement date, expiration date, renewal trigger, whether the BAA is on file, and the date of the most recent security assessment. A sixty-day auto-flag column highlights agreements approaching expiration. The renewal calendar view shows all upcoming deadlines in one place so you do not discover a lapsed BAA when the investigator asks for it.

Tab 4 — Staff Training Compliance

HIPAA requires that all workforce members receive training on privacy and security policies within a reasonable time after hire, and that training is documented with attestation. This tab tracks: employee name, role, training topic, date completed, next due date, quiz or competency score, and attestation status. The dashboard flags any employee whose training has lapsed and calculates the overall practice training compliance percentage — OCR expects ninety percent or higher. Annual refresher training for all staff, documented in this tab, is one of the strongest "good faith compliance effort" signals you can show an auditor.

Tab 5 — Security Risk Assessment (SRA) Tracker

The SRA is not a one-time exercise — it must be reviewed and updated at least annually, or whenever there is a material change to your practice's technology environment (new EHR, new patient portal, office move, server migration). This tab tracks: risk identified, category (administrative, physical, technical), likelihood rating, impact rating, current controls, mitigation plan, responsible person, target date, and status. The SRA must cover all locations where PHI is stored or transmitted — on-premises servers, cloud services, workstations, mobile devices, email, fax, and paper records. A practice that completed an SRA three years ago and has not touched it since will fail an OCR audit as thoroughly as a practice that never did one.

The Five-Factor Breach Risk Assessment — Built Into Tab 2

The Breach Notification Rule requires that every impermissible use or disclosure of PHI be evaluated under a five-factor risk assessment to determine whether the incident constitutes a notifiable breach. The five factors, as defined by HHS/OCR guidance, are:

  1. Nature and extent of PHI involved: What types of identifiers were exposed? Clinical information, financial data, and sensitive diagnoses carry higher risk than demographic data alone.
  2. The unauthorized person who used the PHI or to whom the disclosure was made: Was it another healthcare provider (who has independent HIPAA obligations) or an unknown third party? A misdirected fax to another doctor's office is different from a laptop stolen from a coffee shop.
  3. Whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed: Was the email opened? Was the laptop accessed? If there is forensic evidence that PHI was not actually viewed, the risk score drops.
  4. Extent to which the risk to the PHI has been mitigated: Did you recover the device? Did the recipient confirm deletion? Was the fax retrieved and destroyed? Document every mitigation action taken.
  5. The persons involved: Internal workforce member versus external party. A nurse who accidentally accessed the wrong chart and immediately reported it is a different risk profile from an unknown external actor.

Each factor is scored on a one-to-five scale in the spreadsheet. The composite score translates to a probability assessment: low (notification not required, but documentation is), medium (notification required — affected individuals plus HHS), or high (notification required — affected individuals, HHS, and media if five hundred or more affected). The critical documentation rule: run the five-factor assessment for EVERY incident, even if the conclusion is low probability with no notification required. OCR wants to see the thinking — the absence of an assessment for an incident that did not result in notification is itself a red flag that the practice is not conducting assessments at all.

The BA Agreement Renewal Calendar — Never Miss an Expiration Again

Business associate agreements do not renew automatically in most cases — and the vendors who send BAAs with auto-renewal clauses are often the same vendors whose agreements have not been reviewed in five years and still reference the 2013 Omnibus Rule as "recent." The renewal calendar in Tab 3 works on a simple principle: every BAA has an expiration date, and sixty days before that date, the cell turns yellow. It turns red at thirty days. At zero days, the agreement has lapsed — and every day the practice operates without a current BAA is a separate violation period.

For an eight-to-twelve vendor practice, this means tracking eight to twelve separate deadlines. A spreadsheet that auto-sorts by days-to-expiration makes it trivially easy: attack the reds first, then the yellows. The renewal process itself — contacting the vendor, reviewing the updated agreement for scope changes, obtaining signature, and filing — takes roughly two to four weeks. The sixty-day warning window gives you time to complete the process before the agreement lapses. This is not complicated — but without a tracking system, it is invisible until the investigator asks.

Frequently asked questions

What HIPAA documentation does a medical practice need to maintain?

A medical practice must maintain: patient authorization logs tracking every disclosure of PHI, a breach incident log with five-factor risk assessments for every impermissible access or disclosure, business associate agreements for every vendor handling PHI (eight to twelve for a typical practice), staff HIPAA training records with attestation and completion dates, and a security risk assessment reviewed at least annually. The free spreadsheet in this guide covers all five categories in a single workbook designed to match what OCR data request letters ask for.

How do I conduct a HIPAA security risk assessment?

An SRA follows the HHS/ONC SRA Tool methodology: identify every location where PHI is stored or transmitted (EHR, cloud backup, workstations, mobile devices, email, fax, paper records), assess threats and vulnerabilities for each location, rate likelihood and impact on a one-to-five scale, document current security controls, and create a remediation plan for gaps. The free spreadsheet includes an SRA tracker tab that walks through each step — the framework matches what an OCR auditor will check for, and completing it is strong evidence of a compliance program in operation.

What is a business associate agreement and who needs one?

A business associate agreement (BAA) is a legally required contract between a covered entity and any vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI. This includes your EHR vendor, billing service, shredding company, IT support, cloud backup, email provider, patient portal, answering service, collection agency, and transcription service. A practice that has not identified and documented BA agreements for every vendor handling PHI is missing the single most commonly cited HIPAA violation — and the free BAA tracker tab in the spreadsheet makes this gap visible in minutes.

How long do I need to keep HIPAA compliance documentation?

HIPAA requires retention of compliance documentation for six years from the date of creation or the date when it was last in effect, whichever is later. This applies to authorization logs, breach assessments, BA agreements, training records, and SRA documentation. The free spreadsheet is designed to be printed or exported as PDF monthly and archived — the printed dashboards, stacked in a binder by year, are exactly what an auditor expects to see and are admissible as evidence of a compliance program in continuous operation.

What triggers a HIPAA breach notification requirement?

A breach notification is triggered when impermissible access, use, or disclosure of unsecured PHI occurs and the incident does not fall under a regulatory exception. You must conduct a five-factor risk assessment: nature of PHI, who accessed it, whether PHI was actually acquired or viewed, extent of mitigation, and persons involved. Low probability of compromise means notification is not required but the assessment must be documented. Medium or high probability requires notification to affected individuals within sixty days and to HHS/OCR within the same timeframe (or annually for breaches under five hundred individuals). Breaches of five hundred or more also require media notification. The five-factor assessment is built into Tab 2 of the free spreadsheet.
