Why job shop scheduling is harder than anyone admits

Most scheduling advice assumes you are running a flow shop: same routing, same operations, predictable cycle times. You run parts through the saw, then the mill, then deburr, then ship. In a job shop, every job has a different routing. Job 1437 goes saw, mill, lathe, heat treat, grind, ship. Job 1438 goes lathe, mill, deburr, ship. Job 1439 goes saw, mill, outside anodize, inspection, ship. The routing variability is what makes scheduling impossible to automate with a simple Gantt chart — and why most software fails. Four variables make job shop scheduling uniquely hard:

  1. Variable routings. Every job has a different sequence of operations on different machines. One job skips the lathe entirely. Another goes through the lathe twice. Standard scheduling algorithms assume a flow.
  2. Changeover cost. Going from steel to aluminum on the same CNC mill costs thirty to sixty minutes of cleanup time. The sequence of jobs on each machine matters — and if you ignore it, you lose three to eight hours per machine per week in changeover downtime.
  3. Outside processing black holes. You send parts to anodize, heat treat, or plating. They come back in a window — five to seven days, sometimes ten. You need to schedule machines for their return, but the return date is a range, not a date. If the parts arrive early, the machine sits idle. If they arrive late, the machine is idle waiting. Either way, you lose capacity.
  4. The hot-job disruption loop. A customer calls Tuesday morning: "We need fifty pieces by Friday." You say yes. You squeeze it onto the lathe Wednesday, the mill Thursday, inspection Friday. Two jobs that were due Friday — and were on track — now start on Friday afternoon and ship next Wednesday. Nobody called those customers. They called you.

The four variables compound: every hot job triggers a changeover chain that ripples through outside processing uncertainty, pushing jobs whose routings were already fragile. A whiteboard cannot model this. A spreadsheet can — if it is built for the problem.

The machine load calendar: your whiteboard, digitized

This is the core of the spreadsheet. It replaces the magnetic whiteboard — the one that takes three hours every Sunday to rearrange and gets wiped when someone leans against it. The machine load calendar has one row per machine and columns for each day of the week. Each cell shows which jobs are scheduled on that machine that day, with estimated hours. Color-coded capacity indicators tell you at a glance whether the machine is fine (green, eighty percent or below), tight (yellow, eighty to one hundred percent), or overloaded (red, above one hundred percent).

If the CNC mill shows red on Wednesday, you do not need to finish reading an email to know there is a problem — the color tells you before the words do. Sort jobs within each cell by due-date priority and the most urgent work floats to the top of the machine's queue automatically.

The due-date priority score: stop guessing which job is hottest

When you have forty open orders and every customer thinks theirs is the priority, you need an objective ranking. The due-date priority score combines three factors into a single number:

  • Days until due — fewer days remaining equals higher urgency
  • Remaining operations count — more operations left equals higher urgency (more chances to be delayed)
  • Estimated remaining hours — more hours equals higher urgency (the job needs more machine time)

The formula: priority score equals (one divided by days-until-due plus one) times remaining-operations times estimated-remaining-hours. Sort the entire job list by this score descending and the hottest jobs float to the top — no debate, no "but my customer is more important," just math. Update it every Monday and the ranking self-corrects as jobs progress and due dates approach.

The what-if insert: the feature that does not exist in free tools

This is the single most-requested scheduling feature in job shops — and it does not exist in any free tool. At the bottom of the machine load calendar, a row labeled "WHAT-IF INSERT." Enter the new job's operations, hours per operation, and due date. The spreadsheet recalculates machine loading for every machine affected, changes the capacity colors where overloads occur, and highlights which existing jobs just slipped past their due dates — and by how many days.

Before you say "yes we can rush it" to a customer, you now have data. The sheet tells you: "Accepting this job pushes Job 1452 from Friday to the following Wednesday on the mill, and Job 1467 from Thursday to Monday on the lathe. Contact these two customers before you accept the rush." That is the decision-support your scheduler needs — the one who also does AP, answers the phone, and was about to say yes because she did not have time to calculate the consequences.

A small reference table at the bottom of the scheduling sheet tracks the setup time cost of every material-to-material transition on each machine. It looks like this:

When you schedule multiple steel jobs back-to-back on the same machine on the same day, you avoid the forty-five-minute transition to aluminum and back. Across twelve machines running five days a week, grouping same-material jobs typically recovers two to five hours of changeover time per week. At a shop rate of eighty dollars per hour, that is roughly one hundred sixty to four hundred dollars of recovered capacity — per week, every week — from a single 5-by-5 reference table.

The daily dispatch list: what the machinist sees

The machine load calendar is for the scheduler. The dispatch list is for the machinist. One printable page per machine per day: what runs, in what order, with setup notes ("fixture A is already on the machine from yesterday — do not remove"). Tape it to the machine. The machinist knows what to run next without walking to the office. When the Friday rush job arrives Thursday afternoon, nobody needs to stop what they are doing — the dispatch list already has tomorrow's sequence, and the what-if insert already told the scheduler what gets bumped.

The Monday 20-minute reschedule: your wife gets her Sunday back

Every Monday morning, in this order:

  1. Update completed operations from last week — mark jobs as moved to their next operation
  2. Re-score due-date priorities — the formula auto-updates as days-until-due decreases
  3. Adjust the machine load calendar — shift unfinished work to this week, fill capacity
  4. Check outside-processing returns — any parts due back this week? Schedule the receiving machine
  5. Print new dispatch lists — one per machine, tape to machine

This is the ritual that replaces three hours of whiteboard rearrangement every Sunday. Twenty minutes, Monday morning, data-driven. The owner's wife — who also does AP, AR, and answers the phone — gets her Sunday back. That alone is worth a spreadsheet.

When the sheet stops working

This spreadsheet works for job shops running up to fifteen machines and roughly one hundred open orders. You will know you have outgrown it when:

  • You need real-time WIP visibility feeding into the schedule. The spreadsheet is updated weekly — if you need daily or shift-level updates because jobs move too fast, you need a system, not a sheet
  • You have more than fifteen machines. The calendar gets too wide to scan in one glance
  • You need cost accounting per job per operation feeding into the schedule. When you start asking "what does it cost to schedule this job on the mill instead of the older machine?" you need MES software
  • Multiple schedulers need to update the schedule simultaneously. A spreadsheet is single-user by design. Two schedulers editing at once means overwritten data

At that point, you are ready for APS or MES software — the category, not a specific vendor. The honest breakpoint keeps the page credible: this sheet solves a real problem for a real size of shop, and when you outgrow it, you will know exactly why and what you need next.

How do I calculate machine loading in a spreadsheet?

Machine loading equals total estimated hours assigned to a machine for a given day. For each job scheduled on that machine, add its estimated hours. Loading percentage equals assigned hours divided by available hours (typically eight per shift). Color-code: green at eighty percent or below, yellow at eighty to one hundred percent, red above one hundred percent. Red means jobs will spill into the next day, creating a cascade — fix it before the shift starts, not during.

What is changeover time and how much does it cost?

Changeover time is the downtime when a machine switches between different materials or setups. Going from steel to aluminum on a CNC mill costs thirty to sixty minutes in chip cleanup, coolant adjustment, and fixture changes. In a twelve-machine shop, three hours of changeover per week wastes twenty thousand to fifty thousand dollars per year in lost spindle time. Grouping same-material jobs on the same machine on the same day — guided by a simple changeover matrix — typically cuts changeover time by forty to fifty percent with no investment beyond better sequencing.
