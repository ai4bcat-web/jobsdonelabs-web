What denied claims actually cost your practice

Every practice manager knows denials are a problem. What almost nobody calculates is the dollar figure. Let us do the math.

The industry average denial rate is 5-15% of claims submitted. For a $1.2 million primary care practice submitting roughly 8,000 claims per year, an 8% denial rate means 640 denied claims annually. At an average allowed amount of $150 per claim, that is $96,000 in denied revenue.

Not all denials are appealable — some are legitimate (patient was not covered, service was not a benefit). But industry data from the Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) shows that roughly 65% of denials are appealable. Of those, 60-70% are overturned on appeal when properly documented. That means:

  • $96,000 in denials × 65% appealable = $62,400 in potentially recoverable claims
  • $62,400 × 65% appeal success rate = $40,560 in actual recovery potential
  • Cost to appeal: roughly $45/hour biller time × 60 minutes per appeal × 60 appeals = $2,700 in labor
  • Net recovery: $37,860 to the bottom line — from claims you were going to write off.

Here is the part nobody talks about: Most small practices do not even attempt 60% of these appeals. Not because the appeal would fail — because the 180-day window closes before anyone pulls the denial out of the filing cabinet. The money is not lost because the payer said no. It is lost because nobody had a system to say yes in time.

The 5 most common denial reasons — and exactly how to appeal each one

Denial reason codes look like alphabet soup, but 80% of denials fall into just five categories. Here is what each one means and the specific documentation that gets it overturned.

Code Meaning How to appeal
CO-16 Medical necessity — payer says the service was not medically necessary Send the clinical notes from the encounter, plus a citation from a peer-reviewed guideline or the payer's own medical policy that supports the service. If the payer has an LCD (Local Coverage Determination) for the CPT code, quote it directly. Template letter included below.
CO-22 Coordination of benefits — payer thinks another insurer is primary Send the other payer's Explanation of Benefits (EOB) showing they processed and paid as secondary. If the patient has no other coverage, send a signed statement from the patient confirming no other insurance. This is the fastest appeal — most CO-22 denials are resolved in one submission.
CO-50 Not covered — payer says the service is not a plan benefit Check the payer's LCD or NCD (National Coverage Determination) for the CPT code. If the service is listed as covered with specific criteria, document how the patient met those criteria. If it is genuinely not covered, check whether an ABN (Advance Beneficiary Notice) was signed — if so, bill the patient. If not, this one is a write-off.
CO-11 Diagnosis mismatch — the diagnosis code does not support the procedure code Send corrected coding with supporting documentation from the encounter. This often happens when the provider documents a specific condition but the coder selects a non-specific ICD-10 code. Have the provider add an addendum to the note with the more specific diagnosis, then resubmit with the corrected ICD-10.
CO-29 Timely filing — payer says the claim was submitted past the deadline This is the hardest appeal, but not impossible. Pull your clearinghouse report showing the original submission date with timestamp. If it was submitted on time and the payer lost it, the clearinghouse report is your proof. If it was genuinely late, document the reason (EHR outage, staff turnover) and request a one-time exception — some payers grant these, especially for established practices.

The 15-minute weekly denial huddle

The tracker only works if someone looks at it. Here is the Monday-morning process that takes 15 minutes and recovers $30K-40K per year.

Weekly denial huddle agenda

  1. Biller pulls the tracker and sorts by "days remaining" — ascending. The claims closest to expiration float to the top.
  2. Flag the 3 highest-value claims expiring this month. These are the ones where the appeal deadline window closes within 30 days AND the billed amount is significant (typically $200+). If there are 12 claims expiring, you cannot appeal all 12 this week — pick the 3 with the biggest dollars attached.
  3. Office manager reviews and allocates. Assign each flagged claim to a biller or, if the appeal is complex (medical necessity with extensive documentation), to an outsourced appeal service. Write the assigned person's initials in the tracker's "status" column.
  4. Review last week's submitted appeals. Any responses come back? Update the tracker — approved, denied-at-second-level, or pending.
  5. Update the dashboard. Note the week-over-week change in total-denials-at-risk. The trending number is what tells you whether your denial rate is improving or getting worse.

Before/after: A family practice in Ohio went from a 40% appeal rate to 85% in three months using this exact system. Their biller had been prioritizing denials by "which ones she remembered" — which meant the highest-dollar denials from 90 days ago were forgotten while she worked on yesterday's $45 denial. The tracker flipped the priority order: oldest claims first, highest-dollar within the oldest. Recovery jumped from $14K/year to $41K/year. Same biller, same hours — different system.

The 5 RCM metrics that tell you whether your practice is healthy or bleeding

Revenue cycle management is not complicated — it is just invisible. Most practice managers cannot answer "what is our net collection rate?" without pulling a report. The five metrics below are the answer. Track them monthly. Post them on a wall if you have to. The act of measuring changes behavior.

The five metrics you need to track every month

  1. Net collection rate (target: 95% or above; red flag: below 92%). This is the percentage of every dollar you are contractually entitled to collect that actually lands in the bank. It accounts for payer contractual adjustments — so it measures what you collected against what you should have collected, not against your chargemaster. Formula: (Payments Received) / (Total Expected Reimbursement After Contractuals) × 100. A 92% rate on a $1.2M practice means $96,000 in uncollected revenue — denials you did not appeal, underpayments you did not catch, patient balances you did not pursue. The difference between 92% and 98% is $72,000 — collected or uncollected — purely from revenue cycle discipline.
  2. Days in AR (target: under 35 days; red flag: above 50 days). This is the average number of days from the date of service until payment arrives. Formula: (Total AR / Average Daily Charges). If your total AR is $87,000 and your average daily charges are $2,500, your days in AR is 34.8. Above 50 days means something structural is wrong — a specific payer is dragging, claims are getting stuck in "pending" status, or your billing team is submitting late. A practice with 52 days in AR is financing its payers' cash flow, and the practice is the one paying interest on the line of credit.
  3. Denial rate (target: under 5%; red flag: above 8%). Denied claims divided by total claims submitted. But do not track it as one number — track it per payer. If your overall denial rate is 6% but UnitedHealthcare denies at 12% and Blue Cross at 2%, the problem is UnitedHealthcare's front-end process (authorizations, medical necessity documentation), not your billing team. A payer-specific denial rate tells you where to focus — you cannot fix "denials" generically.
  4. Clean claim rate (target: 95% or above; red flag: below 90%). The percentage of claims accepted on first submission — no rejection, no denial, no request for additional information. This is a leading indicator for denial rate. If your clean claim rate drops from 95% to 88%, your denial rate will rise in 30-60 days — because the same root causes (bad eligibility checks, missing prior auths, incorrect coding) that cause rejections today become denials tomorrow. Fix the clean claim rate and the denial rate follows.
  5. Point-of-service collection rate (target: 60% or above; red flag: below 40%). The percentage of patient-responsible amounts (copays, deductibles, coinsurance) collected at the time of the visit. Every dollar not collected at check-out costs an average of $15 in billing and collections effort to recover later. A practice collecting 25% at time of service spends $14,000 more per year on patient collections than a practice collecting 65% — for the exact same patient volume. The front desk is your highest-leverage collection tool, and most practices do not measure it.

The five-number monthly check: On the first Monday of every month, write down these five numbers: Net Collection Rate ___, Days in AR ___, Denial Rate ___, Clean Claim Rate ___, POS Collection Rate ___. Compare to the previous month. Any metric that moved in the wrong direction gets a named owner and a 30-day fix-it project. The dashboard makes this a 30-second exercise. The alternative — running four reports, cross-referencing dates, and giving up halfway through — is what most practices do today.

The payer slow-pay leaderboard

Every practice has a story about a payer that takes 90 days to pay. But almost no practice has the data to prove it. The leaderboard below ranks every payer by the metric that costs you the most: cash flow.

How to build it: On Tab 4 of the dashboard, list every payer you contract with — typically 8-15 for a small practice. For each payer, pull the last six months of claims data and calculate four numbers:

Metric What it measures Formula
Average days to pay From claim submission to payment posting — the cash-flow metric. A payer averaging 52 days vs. 18 days is costing you 34 days of float per claim. Sum of (payment date minus submission date) / number of claims paid
Denial rate Percent of claims denied on first submission — the frustration metric. A 14% denial rate means 1 in 7 claims requires rework. Denied claims / total claims submitted per payer
Underpayment rate Percent of claims paid below the contracted rate — the hidden-revenue metric. A 3% underpayment rate on a payer doing $80K/year is $2,400 in unrecovered underpayments annually. (Sum of contracted rate minus actual paid) / sum of contracted rates
Claim rejection rate Percent of claims rejected before processing (missing info, eligibility) — the front-end quality metric. High rejection means your eligibility checks or claim scrubbing need work. Rejected claims / total claims submitted per payer

What to do with the leaderboard:

  • Top 3 worst payers (longest days to pay, highest denial rate): Two options. Option A — front-end process fix. Review what happens BEFORE the claim is submitted to this payer. Is eligibility checked 3 days ahead or on the morning of the visit? Is prior authorization secured before the procedure or after the denial arrives? Most slow-pay problems are front-end problems wearing a back-end disguise. Option B — contract renegotiation. Armed with six months of data showing UnitedHealthcare pays in 52 days while Blue Cross pays in 15, you have leverage to request faster payment terms at renewal. Most practices never ask because they cannot prove.
  • Bottom 3 best payers (fastest payment, lowest denials): These are the payers you want more of. When you are deciding which new insurance panels to join, or which existing panels to market to prospective patients, prioritize the fast payers. If Cigna pays in 14 days with a 2% denial rate and Humana takes 60 days with an 11% denial rate, every new Cigna patient is worth more to the practice than every new Humana patient — even if the contracted rates are identical.
  • The renegotiation file: Send the leaderboard to your billing team and ask them to update it monthly. When a payer contract comes up for renewal, print the leaderboard and bring it to the negotiation. "Your average days to pay is 48 — our practice average across all payers is 31. Our ask: contract language requiring payment within 30 days or automatic 1.5% monthly interest on balances past 30." Most practices do not know their own data. The ones who show up with it get better terms.

The leaderboard pays for itself in 90 days: The first time you catch a payer systematically underpaying by 3% on a $60,000 annual volume — and recover the $1,800 — the dashboard has already paid for the year. The second time, it is pure profit. The third time, you start wondering why you did not build this five years ago.

Frequently asked questions

How do I track denied medical claims without buying software?

Use a free Google Sheets spreadsheet with columns for patient name, claim number, denial date, denial reason code, appeal deadline (auto-calculated based on payer — 180 days for commercial, 120 for Medicare), appeal status, and recovered amount. Add conditional formatting to highlight claims with fewer than 30 days remaining. This replaces the filing-cabinet-and-sticky-note approach most small practices rely on, and it costs nothing.

What are the most common medical claim denial reasons and how do I appeal them?

The five most common denial reason codes are CO-16 (medical necessity — appeal with clinical notes and guideline citations), CO-22 (coordination of benefits — send the other payer's EOB), CO-50 (not covered — check the payer's LCD/NCD and document medical necessity), CO-11 (diagnosis mismatch — send corrected coding with provider addendum), and CO-29 (timely filing — provide clearinghouse report showing original submission timestamp). Each requires specific documentation; template letters for all five are in the tracker.

How long do I have to appeal a denied medical claim?

Commercial insurance: typically 180 days from the denial date on the remittance advice. Medicare: 120 days from the remittance advice date. Medicaid: varies by state, typically 30-90 days. Always verify the specific deadline in the payer's provider manual — it is printed on the remittance advice. Missing the deadline converts the denial into a permanent write-off with no further appeal rights.

How much money does a medical practice lose on unworked denials?

A $1.2M practice with an 8% denial rate loses approximately $96,000 in denied claims annually. About 65% of those ($62,400) are appealable, and 60-70% are overturned on appeal — meaning $37,000-$43,000 is recoverable. After subtracting biller labor for appeals (~$2,700), the net recovery is roughly $34,000-$40,000. Most practices capture less than half of this because denials expire before anyone appeals them.
