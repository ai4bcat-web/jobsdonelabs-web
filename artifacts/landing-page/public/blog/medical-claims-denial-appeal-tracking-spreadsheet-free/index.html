What denied claims actually cost your practice

Every practice manager knows denials are a problem. What almost nobody calculates is the dollar figure. Let's do the math.

The industry average denial rate is 5-15% of claims submitted. For a $1.2 million primary care practice submitting roughly 8,000 claims per year, an 8% denial rate means 640 denied claims annually. At an average allowed amount of $150 per claim, that's $96,000 in denied revenue.

Not all denials are appealable — some are legitimate (patient wasn't covered, service wasn't a benefit). But industry data from the Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) shows that roughly 65% of denials are appealable. Of those, 60-70% are overturned on appeal when properly documented. That means:

  • $96,000 in denials × 65% appealable = $62,400 in potentially recoverable claims
  • $62,400 × 65% appeal success rate = $40,560 in actual recovery potential
  • Cost to appeal: roughly $45/hour biller time × 60 minutes per appeal × 60 appeals = $2,700 in labor
  • Net recovery: $37,860 to the bottom line — from claims you were going to write off.

Here's the part nobody talks about: Most small practices don't even attempt 60% of these appeals. Not because the appeal would fail — because the 180-day window closes before anyone pulls the denial out of the filing cabinet. The money isn't lost because the payer said no. It's lost because nobody had a system to say yes in time.

The 5 most common denial reasons — and exactly how to appeal each one

Denial reason codes look like alphabet soup, but 80% of denials fall into just five categories. Here's what each one means and the specific documentation that gets it overturned.

Code Meaning How to appeal
CO-16 Medical necessity — payer says the service wasn't medically necessary Send the clinical notes from the encounter, plus a citation from a peer-reviewed guideline or the payer's own medical policy that supports the service. If the payer has an LCD (Local Coverage Determination) for the CPT code, quote it directly. Template letter included below.
CO-22 Coordination of benefits — payer thinks another insurer is primary Send the other payer's Explanation of Benefits (EOB) showing they processed and paid as secondary. If the patient has no other coverage, send a signed statement from the patient confirming no other insurance. This is the fastest appeal — most CO-22 denials are resolved in one submission.
CO-50 Not covered — payer says the service isn't a plan benefit Check the payer's LCD or NCD (National Coverage Determination) for the CPT code. If the service is listed as covered with specific criteria, document how the patient met those criteria. If it's genuinely not covered, check whether an ABN (Advance Beneficiary Notice) was signed — if so, bill the patient. If not, this one's a write-off.
CO-11 Diagnosis mismatch — the diagnosis code doesn't support the procedure code Send corrected coding with supporting documentation from the encounter. This often happens when the provider documents a specific condition but the coder selects a non-specific ICD-10 code. Have the provider add an addendum to the note with the more specific diagnosis, then resubmit with the corrected ICD-10.
CO-29 Timely filing — payer says the claim was submitted past the deadline This is the hardest appeal, but not impossible. Pull your clearinghouse report showing the original submission date with timestamp. If it was submitted on time and the payer lost it, the clearinghouse report is your proof. If it was genuinely late, document the reason (EHR outage, staff turnover) and request a one-time exception — some payers grant these, especially for established practices.

The 15-minute weekly denial huddle

The tracker only works if someone looks at it. Here's the Monday-morning process that takes 15 minutes and recovers $30K-40K per year.

Weekly denial huddle agenda

  1. Biller pulls the tracker and sorts by "days remaining" — ascending. The claims closest to expiration float to the top.
  2. Flag the 3 highest-value claims expiring this month. These are the ones where the appeal deadline window closes within 30 days AND the billed amount is significant (typically $200+). If there are 12 claims expiring, you can't appeal all 12 this week — pick the 3 with the biggest dollars attached.
  3. Office manager reviews and allocates. Assign each flagged claim to a biller or, if the appeal is complex (medical necessity with extensive documentation), to an outsourced appeal service. Write the assigned person's initials in the tracker's "status" column.
  4. Review last week's submitted appeals. Any responses come back? Update the tracker — approved, denied-at-second-level, or pending.
  5. Update the dashboard. Note the week-over-week change in total-denials-at-risk. The trending number is what tells you whether your denial rate is improving or getting worse.

Before/after: A family practice in Ohio went from a 40% appeal rate to 85% in three months using this exact system. Their biller had been prioritizing denials by "which ones she remembered" — which meant the highest-dollar denials from 90 days ago were forgotten while she worked on yesterday's $45 denial. The tracker flipped the priority order: oldest claims first, highest-dollar within the oldest. Recovery jumped from $14K/year to $41K/year. Same biller, same hours — different system.

When to outsource vs. handle in-house

Not every practice needs to outsource denial management. But at a certain volume, in-house appeals hit diminishing returns — the biller is spending so much time appealing that new claims aren't getting submitted.

Monthly denial volume Recommended approach Estimated cost
Below $30K/month in denials Spreadsheet + in-house biller. The volume is manageable — 20-40 denials per month — and the biller can handle appeals alongside regular billing duties with a structured tracker. $0 for the spreadsheet; biller time already budgeted
$30K–$100K/month Dedicated denial manager or part-time appeal specialist. At this volume (60-150 denials/month), appeals become a full-time function. A dedicated person pays for themselves at a 3:1 recovery-to-salary ratio. $35K–$50K/year salary, offset by $100K–$150K in recovery
$100K+/month Outsource to an RCM vendor. Vendors like Waystar, Navicure, and Kareo have dedicated appeal teams, payer-specific expertise, and software that auto-generates appeal letters. They typically charge a percentage of recovered revenue (3-8%). 3-8% of recovered revenue; typically yields higher overturn rates than in-house at scale

The golden rule: Whatever approach you choose, run the tracker first. Before you hire a denial manager or sign an RCM contract, use the spreadsheet for 90 days. Measure: how many denials do you actually receive per month? What's your current appeal rate? What's your current recovery rate? Those three numbers tell you exactly what kind of help you need — and whether it'll pay for itself.

Frequently asked questions

How do I track denied medical claims without buying software?

Use a free Google Sheets spreadsheet with columns for patient name, claim number, denial date, denial reason code, appeal deadline (auto-calculated based on payer — 180 days for commercial, 120 for Medicare), appeal status, and recovered amount. Add conditional formatting to highlight claims with fewer than 30 days remaining. This replaces the filing-cabinet-and-sticky-note approach most small practices rely on, and it costs nothing.

What are the most common medical claim denial reasons and how do I appeal them?

The five most common denial reason codes are CO-16 (medical necessity — appeal with clinical notes and guideline citations), CO-22 (coordination of benefits — send the other payer's EOB), CO-50 (not covered — check the payer's LCD/NCD and document medical necessity), CO-11 (diagnosis mismatch — send corrected coding with provider addendum), and CO-29 (timely filing — provide clearinghouse report showing original submission timestamp). Each requires specific documentation; template letters for all five are in the tracker.

How long do I have to appeal a denied medical claim?

Commercial insurance: typically 180 days from the denial date on the remittance advice. Medicare: 120 days from the remittance advice date. Medicaid: varies by state, typically 30-90 days. Always verify the specific deadline in the payer's provider manual — it's printed on the remittance advice. Missing the deadline converts the denial into a permanent write-off with no further appeal rights.

How much money does a medical practice lose on unworked denials?

A $1.2M practice with an 8% denial rate loses approximately $96,000 in denied claims annually. About 65% of those ($62,400) are appealable, and 60-70% are overturned on appeal — meaning $37,000-$43,000 is recoverable. After subtracting biller labor for appeals (~$2,700), the net recovery is roughly $34,000-$40,000. Most practices capture less than half of this because denials expire before anyone appeals them.

When should I outsource medical claim denial appeals?

Below $30K/month in denials: handle in-house with a spreadsheet. Between $30K-$100K/month: hire a dedicated denial manager or part-time appeal specialist.
