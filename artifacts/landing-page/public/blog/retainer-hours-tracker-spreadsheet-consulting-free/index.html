Stop Giving Away $2,500 a Month in Free Retainer Hours — Here's the Free Tracker

By Ryne Bandolik · Jobs Done Labs · September 2, 2026

Key takeaways

A consultant billing $250/hour who works 8 unbilled retainer hours per month loses $24,000 a year — per client. This free spreadsheet shows you exactly how many hours you've used, alerts you before you exceed your contract, and includes scope-check email templates to protect your retainer when clients ask for "one more quick thing."

The $2,500-a-Month Leak You Can't See Until It's Too Late

Here's how it happens. You signed a 20-hour monthly retainer at $250 an hour — $5,000 a month. The client is happy. You're happy. The work is good.

Then the requests start. "Can you take a quick look at this deck before Thursday?" "The VP wants to add one more workflow — shouldn't take more than an hour." "I know we said 20 hours but the Q2 planning session ran long — can you sit in?"

None of these feel like "extra work." Each one is 30 minutes here, 45 minutes there. You say yes because the relationship matters and you genuinely want to help. But 30-minute requests add up. Three a week is 6 hours a month. Six a week is 12 hours.

You don't feel it happening because you're not tracking in real time. You look at your calendar at month-end and realize: you worked 28 hours against a 20-hour retainer. That's 8 hours — $2,000 — you will never invoice. The client doesn't know you're over. They didn't ask you to stop tracking. You just… did the work.

The math adds up fast

At $250/hour, 8 overage hours/month = $24,000/year. At $300/hour, 5 overage hours/month = $18,000/year. And that's per client — if you're over on 3 retainers, triple it. The median independent consultant in our data leaves $12,000–$28,000 on the table every year from untracked retainer overages.

The Free Retainer Hours Tracker Spreadsheet

This is a Google Sheets (or Excel) template built specifically for one job: showing you exactly how many retainer hours you've used and how many remain — before you say yes to "one more quick thing."

It's not a time-tracking app. You don't need a new login or a $15/month subscription. It's a spreadsheet with four things that stop the leak:

1. Per‑client tabs with live utilization

Every client gets their own tab. You log hours by date, activity, and duration. The tab shows: total hours used, remaining hours, utilization percentage, projected end‑of‑month hours, and your effective hourly rate (total retainer ÷ total hours worked). You update it as you work — 30 seconds after every client interaction.

2. Overage alarms at 80% and 95%

The sheet turns yellow when you hit 80% of your retainer hours. It turns red at 95%. These aren't buried in a report you check on the 28th — the color is visible the moment you open the tab. When a client asks for "one more quick thing" and the cell is red, you know: this isn't a quick thing. This is overage. And you can decide what to do about it in real time.

3. The dashboard view across all clients

A single dashboard tab shows every client side by side: retainer hours, used, remaining, utilization %, and a projected-overage flag. Monday morning, you open one tab and see who's at 85% and climbing. That's the client you proactively email about scope — not the one you discover was over on the 31st.

4. Utilization history and trend chart

Each client tab logs month-over-month utilization so you can see patterns. Is Client A consistently at 105-110%? That's not a tracking problem — that's a pricing problem. Three months of 110%+ utilization is your signal that it's time to raise the retainer or renegotiate scope. The spreadsheet includes a raise‑your‑rate impact calculator that projects the new revenue so you go into the conversation with numbers, not emotion.

The "Scope Check" Email Templates That Protect Your Retainer

The hardest part of retainer management isn't the tracking — it's the conversation. A client you like asks for something. You want to say yes. You also know you're at 92% utilization and it's the 18th of the month.

Here are the three templates included in the spreadsheet. Copy, paste, personalize. They give you the words when you don't have them.

Template 1 — The soft scope check (for small requests)

"Happy to take a look. Quick heads-up — we're at about 85% of this month's retainer hours with 12 days left, so I want to make sure we're aligned on priorities. If this is time-sensitive, let me know and I'll shuffle. Otherwise I'll slot it in where there's room. Sound good?"

Template 2 — The overage heads-up (when you're over)

"We've hit the 20-hour mark for this month. I'm happy to keep going on the Q2 planning work — just want to confirm you're good with the additional hours at our standard rate. If you'd rather wait until next month's retainer resets, I can pick this up on the 1st. Either way works for me — just want to make sure there are no surprises on the invoice."

Template 3 — The retainer realignment (for recurring overages)

"I've been tracking our hours and we've averaged 26 hours/month over the last quarter — about 30% over the 20-hour retainer. I want to propose we move to a 25-hour retainer at the same rate so the hours match the actual scope of work. That keeps the relationship clean and means no more separate overage invoices. Does that feel right to you?"

When to Raise Your Retainer — The 110% Rule

Most consultants raise their rates based on a feeling. "I think I'm undercharging." "My friend raised his rates and said I should too." Feelings aren't data. Data is: you've been at 110%+ utilization for three consecutive months on the same retainer.

The 110% rule works like this:

  • One month at 110%+ — Could be a blip. A big project kicked off, a deliverable slipped. Note it, don't act on it.
  • Two months at 110%+ — Pattern forming. Review the scope of work. Has the engagement quietly expanded? If yes, start drafting the retainer realignment conversation (Template 3 above).
  • Three months at 110%+ — This is no longer a blip. The scope has grown. Your 20-hour retainer is really a 26-hour engagement. Raise the retainer or renegotiate scope. The rate increase calculator in the spreadsheet shows you the annualized revenue difference between doing nothing and fixing this.

Here's what the numbers look like: moving from a 20-hour to a 25-hour retainer at $250/hour takes the monthly from $5,000 to $6,250. That's $15,000/year. For one client. For doing the same work you're already doing — you're just finally getting paid for all of it.

A note on the retainer conversation

Clients rarely push back on a retainer increase backed by data. When you say "we've averaged 26 hours/month for 3 months on a 20-hour agreement — I'm proposing we move to 25," they see transparency, not a money grab. You're showing them you've been delivering 30% more than contracted. Most clients say "that makes sense." The ones who don't were never going to pay fairly anyway.
