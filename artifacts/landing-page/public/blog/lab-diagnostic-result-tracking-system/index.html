That Abnormal Lab Result That Nobody Followed Up On? It Just Cost a Practice $4 Million — Free Lab and Diagnostic Result Tracking System

By Ryne Bandolik · November 12, 2026 · Healthcare and Medical Practices

A patient gets a CT scan for abdominal pain. The radiologist notes a 9mm pulmonary nodule — incidental finding, recommend follow-up CT in 6 months. The report gets filed in the EHR. The primary care physician sees the result, checks normal on the abdominal findings, and never scrolls down to the incidental section. Six months pass. Twelve months. Eighteen months later the patient presents with stage IV lung cancer. The nodule was resectable at 6 months. The missed follow-up just became a malpractice case with a $4.2 million judgment.

This is not an edge case. Missed or delayed diagnosis is the #1 allegation in medical malpractice claims, and failure to follow up on abnormal test results is one of the most common factual patterns behind those claims. The problem is not that results are never seen — it is that they are seen once and never tracked to resolution. A result arrives, someone glances at it, and if it does not scream emergency, it goes into the chart with no system for ensuring the loop gets closed.

The cost of a single missed result One missed abnormal lab or imaging finding can trigger: a malpractice lawsuit (average defense cost: $30,000-$50,000 even if you win; average plaintiff verdict for missed cancer diagnosis: $2-$5 million), a state medical board investigation, loss of patient trust across your entire panel, and MIPS/MACRA quality-score penalties that reduce your Medicare reimbursement. The free tracking spreadsheet in this guide costs nothing and takes 90 minutes to set up. The first result it catches pays for the setup time a thousand times over.

The 3-tier result flagging system

Not every abnormal result requires the same response. A potassium of 6.1 and a vitamin D of 19 are both abnormal — but treating them with the same urgency wastes resources on the wrong problems and lets the truly critical ones blend in with the routine. The 3-tier system categorizes every result by clinical urgency:

TierExamplesProvider ReviewPatient NotificationClosure Deadline
Tier 1: Critical K+ 6.2, troponin 0.8, Hgb 5.1, new pulmonary embolism on CT, acute intracranial hemorrhage, positive blood culture, critical INR > 5.0, new malignancy on pathology Within 2 hours — call provider immediately if they have not seen it Same day, direct phone contact with patient or responsible party. Document who you spoke with, exactly what was said, and the action taken (sent to ER, direct admit, stat follow-up). 24 hours — result must have a documented disposition (admitted, transferred, follow-up scheduled, or patient refused and refusal documented)
Tier 2: Abnormal HbA1c 9.2, LDL 190, eGFR 42, TSH 0.08, CT showing 8mm lung nodule — recommend follow-up in 6 months, mammogram BI-RADS 3 — probably benign, DEXA T-score -2.8, elevated LFTs 3x normal, colonoscopy finding 8mm polyp — pathology pending Within 24 hours Within 72 hours — phone call for results requiring explanation (new diagnosis, follow-up imaging needed, medication change). Portal message acceptable for stable abnormal results with clear next steps. Letter for low-clinical-urgency findings. 7 days for notification, follow-up appointment scheduled within 30 days. Follow-up completion tracked separately — closure only when the follow-up appointment is verified as attended.
Tier 3: Incidental Vitamin D 18, mild anemia (Hgb 11.5 in asymptomatic patient), benign hepatic cyst on CT, thyroid nodule < 1cm — no suspicious features, mild B12 deficiency, borderline lipid elevation in low-risk patient Within 7 days Within 14 days — letter or portal message documenting the finding and stating clearly whether follow-up is needed. If the finding is clinically insignificant, say so explicitly: "This finding does not require any follow-up or treatment." Without this sentence, patients will worry. 30 days — result is documented and patient notified. If the finding requires no follow-up, the loop is closed when the patient receives the notification. If follow-up is recommended (repeat labs in 6 months), add a future-due date to the tracking spreadsheet.
Who decides the tier? The provider always makes the final determination — but your staff can pre-flag results based on lab reference ranges and radiology report keywords. Build a one-page "flagging cheat sheet" for your clinical staff: any result with these keywords (malignancy, hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, critical values, acute) gets Tier 1. These keywords (nodule, elevated, abnormal, recommended follow-up, suspicious) get Tier 2. Everything else starts as Tier 3 and gets escalated if the provider determines it needs faster action. The sheet lives next to the result-review workstation — laminated, single page, impossible to ignore.

Tab 2: Pending Results Dashboard

A single view that shows every open result, sorted by urgency. This is what the practice manager or lead provider looks at every morning. Build with formulas that pull from the Result Log — no manual data entry on this tab:

  • Critical results pending review: COUNTIFS of Tier 1 + Provider Reviewed = No. This number should be zero every morning. If it is not, someone is getting a phone call before 9 AM.
  • Abnormal results not yet notified: Tier 2 + Patient Notified = No + Date Received > 2 days ago. These are your highest risk — the result is known, the patient does not know, and every day of delay increases liability.
  • Overdue follow-ups: Follow-Up Due Date < TODAY() + Follow-Up Completed = No. These patients needed a follow-up appointment that never happened. Someone needs to call them today.
  • Results by provider: Pivot table showing open results per provider. If Dr. Smith has 23 open results and Dr. Jones has 4, you have a workflow problem — not a provider problem. Dr. Smith needs help, not a lecture.
  • Aging summary: Count of open results by days since receipt: 0-3 days, 4-7 days, 8-14 days, 15-30 days, 30+ days. Anything in the 30+ bucket is a potential liability — escalate immediately.

The closed-loop result communication protocol

The tracking spreadsheet is the tool. The protocol is the process. Together they ensure every result travels through six sequential steps — and no step is considered complete until the next one has happened:

  1. Result received → Logged in the Result Log within 24 hours of arriving in the EHR inbox. Result ID assigned. Tier pre-assigned by clinical staff based on flagging cheat sheet.
  2. Provider review → Provider reviews the result, confirms or changes the Tier, assigns the Required Action, sets the Follow-Up Due Date if applicable, and initials the Sign-Off Log. This should happen: Tier 1 within 2 hours, Tier 2 within 24 hours, Tier 3 within 7 days.
  3. Patient notification → Patient is contacted using the method appropriate for the Tier. Discussion is documented in the Notification Log. If the patient cannot be reached, a second and third attempt are made per your practice's policy. After 3 failed attempts, escalate to certified mail.
  4. Follow-up scheduled → If the result requires follow-up (repeat labs, imaging, specialist visit), the appointment is scheduled and confirmed. The Follow-Up Due Date is entered in the Result Log. The patient receives an appointment reminder per your normal process.
  5. Follow-up completed → The appointment is verified as attended. This is the step most practices skip — they schedule the follow-up and consider it done. You must confirm the patient actually showed up. If they no-showed, the loop is not closed — go back to step 3.
  6. Loop closed → Date Closed is entered in the Result Log. The result moves from the Pending Results Dashboard to the closed archive. The patient's chart is updated with the follow-up outcome. Done.
The rule that prevents everything from breaking A result is never considered closed until the Date Closed field is populated. If that field is blank, the result is still open — regardless of how many conversations happened, how many orders were placed, or how confident everyone feels that it was handled. One blank field. That is the entire safety net. Every morning, sort the Result Log by Date Closed (blanks first) and work from the top. In 10 minutes you will know exactly which results need attention and exactly who is responsible for each one.

When to upgrade from a spreadsheet

The spreadsheet system works for practices processing fewer than 150 results per week. Above that volume — or if you are experiencing any of these symptoms — it is time to consider dedicated result-management software or custom automation:

  • Volume exceeds 150 results per week. At this volume, the 15-20 minute daily logging ritual stretches to 45+ minutes, and the spreadsheet becomes difficult to scan for outliers.
  • Multiple locations. When results arrive at three different offices and providers rotate between them, a shared spreadsheet requires meticulous discipline — one person forgetting to log for two days creates blind spots that compound.
  • You have had a near-miss. A critical result sat for 3 days. A follow-up was ordered but never scheduled. A patient transferred care because nobody called with their biopsy results. One near-miss is a warning sign that your current system — spreadsheet or otherwise — is not being followed. Two near-misses mean the system itself is broken.
  • You need EHR integration. If you want results auto-logged, patient notifications auto-generated, and follow-up reminders auto-sent, you have outgrown the manual spreadsheet. This is where custom automation delivers the highest ROI.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I make sure abnormal lab results do not get missed?

The most common failure point is not that results are never seen — it is that they are seen once and not tracked to closure. Implement a 3-tier flagging system: Critical (requires action within 24 hours), Abnormal (requires action within 72 hours), and Incidental (document and notify within 7-14 days). Every result gets logged with a patient identifier, finding, required action, assigned provider, notification date, and closure date. The free tracking spreadsheet in this guide gives you the system pre-built — zero cost, 90 minutes to set up. The closed-loop workflow ensures nothing falls through the cracks: result received, provider reviewed, patient notified, follow-up scheduled, follow-up completed, and loop closed. Sort by the Date Closed column every morning — anything with a blank Closure Date is still open and needs attention.

What is the standard of care for notifying patients of test results?

There is no single federal standard, but case law has established that practices have a duty to notify patients of clinically significant results in a timely manner. The AMA recommends that practices document a clear result-notification policy, communicate the policy to patients, and close the loop on every result — meaning you must confirm the patient actually received and understood the result, not just that you left a voicemail. For critical results, the standard is same-day direct contact with the patient or a responsible party. For abnormal but non-emergent results, most practices aim for 48-72 hour notification. The key legal principle: the practice owns the follow-up — you cannot simply send a letter and assume the patient received it. Document every notification attempt: date, method, who you spoke with, outcome, and whether follow-up was scheduled.

Can I use a spreadsheet to track lab results in a medical practice?

Yes — and for practices processing fewer than 150 results per week, a well-designed spreadsheet is often faster and more reliable than an EHR result-management module that nobody was trained on. The key is that the spreadsheet must have: a unique identifier per result so nothing gets double-logged, automatic priority flagging based on finding severity, clear provider assignment so accountability is unambiguous, a closure date field that makes open results immediately visible, and a regular review cadence — daily for critical and abnormal, weekly for incidentals. The free JobsDone Labs tracking spreadsheet includes all five features plus a provider sign-off log, patient notification log, and pending-results dashboard. HIPAA note: store the spreadsheet on a secure, access-controlled drive with audit logging — not on an unencrypted personal device.

How long should it take to follow up on an abnormal result?

It depends on severity. Critical results should trigger provider review within 2 hours and patient notification the same day. Abnormal results (elevated labs, nodules requiring follow-up imaging, findings that need further workup) should have provider review within 24 hours and patient notification within 72 hours. Incidental findings (benign cysts, anatomical variants of no clinical significance) should be reviewed within 7 days and communicated to the patient with a clear statement that no follow-up is needed — otherwise the patient will worry about a finding you already determined is benign. The most common failure pattern is the result that falls in the middle — abnormal but not critical. The provider sees it, thinks they will address it tomorrow, and tomorrow never comes. The tracking spreadsheet prevents this by flagging any result that has been open longer than 72 hours without a closure date.
