The hidden cost of not knowing your real defect rate

Here's how the math works in a typical 40,000-part/month job shop. The production manager reports 97% yield. The shop floor feels like things are running well — most parts ship on time, customers aren't complaining. But here's what's actually happening:

Operators catch defects during production and rework them on the spot. The reworked parts eventually pass inspection and ship. Those hours of rework labor never get flagged as a quality problem — they just disappear into the shop's labor variance. The scrap that does get counted is what's obvious: the cracked casting, the hole drilled in the wrong location. What doesn't get counted: the 3,600 parts that got reworked last month because a cutting tool was worn 0.004" oversize, or the 2,400 parts that were scrapped because the first-article inspection checklist wasn't followed after a setup change.

The real numbers: 40,000 parts/month × 15% true defect rate (not the 3% you report) = 6,000 defective parts/month. At $12/part material + labor, that's $72,000/month in scrap value. But rework costs MORE than scrap: a reworked part consumes 2–3x the original labor. If 60% of defects are reworked and 40% scrapped: 3,600 reworked × $24/rework cost = $86,400 + 2,400 scrapped × $12 = $28,800. Total: $115,200/month in quality costs that don't appear on any report.

The worst part? Nobody sees this number. The accountant's scrap report arrives 3 weeks after month-end and only captures the parts that were physically thrown away. The rework hours are buried in labor variance. The root cause — a worn tool that should have been changed 400 parts ago — was already replaced and forgotten. By the time the report lands, the machine settings have been changed twice and the operator has rotated to a different cell. The problem is both invisible and unsolvable by the time you learn about it.

First pass yield vs. final yield — the metric your customers actually care about

Here's a distinction that separates shops that know their quality from shops that guess:

  • First pass yield (FPY) = the percentage of parts that pass inspection the FIRST time, before any rework. Formula: Parts OK first pass ÷ Total parts produced.
  • Final yield = the percentage of parts that eventually ship. Formula: (Total produced – Scrapped) ÷ Total produced.

A shop running 82% FPY and 99% final yield sounds impressive — "99% of everything we build ships to the customer!" But here's what that actually means: 17% of every part you produce is reworked. Your customers receive good parts because you're spending a fortune in rework labor making them good. Your margins are being consumed by labor that doesn't add value — it just fixes mistakes.

The gap between FPY and final yield IS your rework cost. If your FPY is 82% and your final yield is 99%, you're reworking 17% of all parts. On 40,000 parts/month: 6,800 parts reworked × $24/rework = $163,200/month in hidden quality cost. That's $1.96 million/year in labor that adds zero value — and it's invisible on your P&L because rework hours are absorbed into the standard labor variance line.

The tracker measures both FPY and final yield, displays the gap between them, and calculates the dollar value of that gap. The target: FPY above 95%. At 95% FPY, your rework drops from 6,800 parts/month to 2,000 — a $115,000/month swing. That's not a process improvement. That's a measurement improvement: once you can see the problem, you can fix it.

Root cause categories and the Pareto principle of defects

80% of your defects come from 20% of root causes. In most small manufacturing shops, the top three are:

Root cause % of defects Fix Cost to fix
1. Tooling wear ~35% Preventative tool change intervals based on tool-life data, not "when it sounds bad" $0 — change tools on a schedule instead of reactively
2. Setup error ~25% First-article inspection checklist: measure the first part off every setup against a checklist of critical dimensions before running production $0 — one printed checklist per machine
3. Operator error ~20% Standardized work instructions with photos, not text from an engineering drawing that nobody can read. Blame the process, not the person. $0 — one afternoon to document and photograph each setup

The spreadsheet's Pareto tab identifies YOUR specific top three within the first month of use. Attack those three — tool change schedule, setup checklist, work instructions — and your defect rate drops 50–70%. This isn't theory. It's the Toyota Production System operating principle applied through a spreadsheet instead of an enterprise MES.

Going beyond the spreadsheet — when quality metrics justify better tools

The spreadsheet is designed to be your first step. It proves the ROI of quality measurement before you spend a dollar on software. Once you've run the tracker for 3–6 months, you'll have hard data to answer the question: "Should we invest in a real QMS?"

The answer depends on what the data tells you:

  • If your FPY is 95%+ and quality cost is under 3% of revenue: The spreadsheet is sufficient. You don't need a QMS — you need to maintain what's working.
  • If your FPY is below 90% and quality cost exceeds 5% of revenue: The spreadsheet has already identified your top root causes. Before buying software, implement the fixes for your top 3 causes (they cost $0). If FPY doesn't improve to 92%+ after 3 months, the root cause is likely systemic — machine capability, material supplier quality, or workforce skill — not a measurement gap. A QMS can help with traceability and audit compliance, but it won't fix a machine that can't hold tolerance.
  • If you need ISO 9001 or AS9100 certification: A QMS like Greenlight Guru, Qualio, or QT9 becomes necessary — not for quality improvement, but for document control, audit trails, and CAPA workflows. Budget $500–1,500/month. The spreadsheet data from your first 3 months gives you the baseline metrics that make QMS implementation faster and more credible with auditors.

But the spreadsheet isn't just a stepping stone. For shops under $5M revenue running 1–2 shifts with under 50 employees, it's often the permanent solution. The problem isn't that you need better software — the problem is that you need any systematic measurement at all. The spreadsheet solves that. Everything else is optimization.

What's the difference between first pass yield and final yield?

How much does rework actually cost per part?

Rework typically costs 2–3x the original per-part labor because it requires re-handling, re-setup, additional inspection, and often expedited processing. If a part costs $12 in material + labor to produce, reworking it costs $24–36. What are the most common root causes of manufacturing defects?

Across small manufacturing shops, the top three root causes follow the Pareto principle: 1. Tooling wear (approximately 35% of defects) — fixed with preventative tool change intervals, 2. Setup error (approximately 25%) — fixed with first-article inspection checklists, 3. Operator error (approximately 20%) — fixed with standardized work instructions, not blame. The quality tracker's Pareto tab identifies your specific top three within the first month of use.

How do I calculate the true cost of scrap and rework?

True quality cost = (scrapped units × unit material + labor cost) + (reworked units × rework labor cost per unit) + (line-down hours × hourly downtime cost). Example: 2,400 scrapped units × $12 = $28,800 + 3,600 reworked units × $24 rework cost = $86,400 + any production downtime from quality stops. The spreadsheet handles these calculations automatically.
