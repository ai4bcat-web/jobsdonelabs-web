The math your practice manager never showed you

Walk through your office and ask: "Which payer reimburses us the best for a 99213?" Most practice owners can't answer. They know United "fights everything" and Aetna "is decent." They don't know the actual dollar difference.

Here's the math they're missing. A 15-patient-per-day practice with 5 major payers. For a standard level-3 established patient visit (99213):

Payer Rate for 99213 Vs. Median
Aetna $104 +$13 above median
Humana $98 +$7 above median
Blue Cross Blue Shield $91 at median
UnitedHealthcare $87 −$4 below median
Cigna $82 −$9 below median

The spread between your highest payer (Aetna, $104) and your lowest (Cigna, $82) is $22 per visit. On paper it's one office visit, one code, one E&M service. In reality it's a $22 gap you'd never notice unless you compared the contracts side by side.

The annual math: 15 patients/day × 5 days × 48 weeks = 3,600 visits/year. If half are level-3 visits: 1,800 × the $13 average rate gap between your bottom two payers and median = $23,400/year in invisible revenue leakage. For a 3-provider practice billing 5,400 level-3 visits/year: $70,200. That's a staff salary — gone, every year, because nobody compared the payer contracts.

And this is just one code. Add level-4 visits (99214), new patient visits (99203-99204), the G2211 complexity add-on, consult codes, and your top procedure codes, and the total gap widens further. The spreadsheet below makes every one of these gaps visible for the first time.

The free payer rate comparison spreadsheet

The spreadsheet has five tabs, each serving a distinct part of the rate comparison workflow. You populate your own EOB data — the spreadsheet does the math.

Dashboard tab — the one-page revenue reality

The dashboard pulls from all other tabs to give you a single view of where your money is and where it's not. It shows: top-20 CPT codes by volume, weighted-average reimbursement rate across all payers, annual revenue projection at current rates, the revenue gap vs. median-rate targets, and a per-payer utilization percentage (what fraction of your visits each payer represents). One number stares back at you: "At current contracted rates, you're leaving $X on the table per year."

Per-Payer tabs — one sheet per insurance contract

Each payer gets a dedicated tab. Header section: payer name, contact name/phone/email, contract effective date, contract renewal date, fee schedule type (Medicare-based percentage, flat fee schedule, or individually negotiated), and contract notes (prior auth requirements, timely filing limit, appeals process). Then the rate grid: your top 20 CPT codes, the contracted allowed amount per code, the Medicare rate for comparison, and the payer's rate as a percentage of Medicare. The renewal date auto-highlights: green if 180+ days out, yellow at 120 days, amber at 90 days, red at 60 days.

Payer Comparison tab — the grid that changes everything

This is the heart of the spreadsheet. Rows = CPT codes. Columns = payers. Each cell = the allowed amount that payer reimburses for that code. Color-scale: green cells = the highest rate for that code across all your payers; red cells = the lowest. A practice owner opens this tab and sees — in 30 seconds — that Cigna is paying $82 for the exact same 99213 Humana pays $98 for. The auto-calculation at the bottom totals current annual revenue at contracted rates and projects what revenue would be if every below-median payer were negotiated to median. That's your negotiation target in dollars.

Contract Renewal Calendar — the deadline you can't afford to miss

Most payer contracts auto-renew with a 90-day opt-out window. If you miss the window, you're locked in for another year at the same rates — rates you now know are below market. This tab tracks: contract start date, renewal date, 90-day opt-out deadline, current rate basis, and your target rate for renegotiation. Auto-highlight rules: renewal deadline within 90 days → amber; within 60 days → red. A separate "Renegotiation Prep" section pulls per-code comparison data so you walk into the call with: "Your rate for 99213 is 15% below the regional median for practices of our size."

Negotiation Prep tab — turn your spreadsheet into a proposal

Select a payer and a date range, and this tab generates a formatted comparison summary ready to export as PDF. It includes: your practice volume for the top codes, the payer's current rates vs. your median rates, the annualized revenue gap, and a suggested target rate for each code. This IS your negotiation documentation — no separate proposal writing needed.

The payer negotiation script — 3 sentences that get a rate review

Most small practices avoid rate renegotiation because they don't know what to say. The fear: the payer will refuse, or worse, drop the contract. In reality, payer provider reps handle rate reviews routinely — they expect them at renewal. What they don't expect from a small practice is data. That's your advantage.

The three-sentence opener (call your provider rep)

  1. "We're reviewing our contract ahead of the [date] renewal. Our internal analysis shows our reimbursement for 99213 is X% below the regional median for practices of our size." — Data-backed, not emotional. You're not complaining. You're informing.
  2. "Can you share the rate schedule you're currently offering to practices with similar volume in our region?" — Not a demand. A question. You're asking for information they have and you don't. If they decline to share rates, that's also data.
  3. "I'd like to open a formal rate review. What documentation do you need from us?" — The spreadsheet IS the documentation. Export the Payer Comparison tab as PDF and attach it. The rep now has your data, your volume, and your requested rates — in a format their contracting team can process.

If the rep says rates are non-negotiable for practices under 10 providers: ask what threshold triggers negotiation eligibility. If it's 10 providers and you're at 3, ask about the carrier's independent practice association (IPA) or clinically integrated network (CIN) — joining one can give you the volume-based negotiating power of a larger group while remaining independent.

If the rep says rates are set regionally and can't be adjusted: ask to speak with the regional contracting manager. Front-line reps have narrow authority; contracting managers have flexibility. The spreadsheet makes you look like the kind of practice that tracks its numbers — and payers negotiate differently with practices that track their numbers.

By the time you realized United was paying 20% below market, you were locked in for another 12 months. That single miss cost a 3-provider practice $42,000.

The Contract Renewal Calendar tab prevents this. It's dead simple: every payer contract gets a row with the renewal date and the opt-out deadline (90 days before renewal). The spreadsheet highlights deadlines automatically. No complex triggers, no calendar integrations — just a column that turns amber when you have 90 days and red when you have 60.

Every Monday morning, you open the tab for 45 seconds. If you see red, you act. If it's all green, you close it. The calendar earns its salary the first time it prevents a missed opt-out.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compare reimbursement rates across different insurance payers?

Start with your top 15-20 CPT codes by volume. Pull the allowed amount from your last 30 EOBs per payer per code — your practice management system or billing reports can export this data. Enter rates into a comparison grid: one row per code, one column per payer, each cell showing the allowed amount. Color-scale from green (highest) to red (lowest). Multiply the per-code gap by your annual volume for that code to get the annual revenue impact. A 3-provider practice typically finds $30K-$70K/year in invisible rate disparities.

What's the best free spreadsheet to track medical practice payer rates?

A purpose-built Google Sheets payer rate comparison spreadsheet with: Dashboard tab showing annual revenue at current rates vs median rates, Per-Payer tabs with fee schedules and contract dates, Payer Comparison tab with per-code color-scaled grid, Contract Renewal Calendar with 90-day auto-highlight warnings, and a Negotiation Prep tab that exports the comparison data as a PDF. This covers the operational need without the $200-$500/month cost of practice management analytics modules for practices under 10 providers.

How much money does a medical practice lose by not comparing payer contracts?

A 15-patient-per-day solo practice with 5 major payers typically loses $23K-$40K per year. The math: if half of 3,600 annual visits are level-3 (99213), and the spread between lowest and median payer rate is $13/visit on that code alone, that's 1,800 x $13 = $23,400. Add level-4 visits, consult codes, and procedure codes, and the gap widens. For a 3-provider practice, the annual revenue leakage from uncompared payer rates tops $70K — enough to hire a full-time biller or nurse.

How do I negotiate better rates with insurance companies as a small practice?

Start 90 days before contract renewal. Call your provider rep with data: "Our internal analysis shows reimbursement for 99213 is 15% below the regional median for practices of our size." Request the rate schedule for similar-volume practices. If declined, request a formal rate review and submit the comparison spreadsheet as documentation. The key difference from standard negotiation: you're citing your own data and volume, not industry averages. A practice billing 1,800 level-3 visits has real negotiating leverage.

Which CPT codes should I compare first?

Start with your top 10-15 codes by volume — they drive 70-80% of revenue. For most primary care practices: 99213 (level-3 established), 99214 (level-4 established), 99203-99204 (new patient visits), G2211 (complexity add-on), and your top 3 procedure codes. Run the comparison on these first — the findings on volume codes dominate the annual impact. Expand to consult codes and preventive codes in a second pass.
