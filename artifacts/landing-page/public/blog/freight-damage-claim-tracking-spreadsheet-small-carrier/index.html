Why 60% of Small-Carrier Cargo Claims Die

Here's the pattern we see over and over: a carrier hauls a load, the consignee finds damage, and the broker denies the claim three weeks later with an email that reads like a form letter. The carrier thinks the broker is being unfair. The broker is just enforcing the paperwork rules that were written into the rate confirmation the carrier signed.

Almost every denied claim we audit dies on one of five procedural misses — not because the damage wasn't real, but because the evidence and the timing weren't right:

  • No photo at delivery. The driver signed a clean POD and drove off. Without photos showing damage at the time of delivery, there's no way to prove the carrier didn't cause it in transit — or worse, after the fact.
  • Unsigned or clean POD. A POD that just has a signature and no notation ("NOTED: 3 cartons crushed") is legally a clean delivery receipt. It says the freight arrived in good order, and it's the hardest piece of evidence to overcome.
  • Missed 48-hour notice window. Brokers require written notice of loss or damage within 48 hours. A phone call doesn't count. Miss it and the claim is procedurally dead before it starts.
  • No inspection report. For concealed damage, there's no delivery-time inspection report and no unpacking report within 5 days — so the carrier has nothing to rebut "you did it after delivery."
  • Filed after the deadline. The Carmack Amendment gives you 9 months to file a formal claim, but brokers routinely write shorter windows into their contracts. Carriers who "get to it when they have time" blow past the deadline they didn't know existed.

Notice what all five have in common: they're decided at the dock or in the first 48 hours — not when the claim is eventually filed. The system below is built backward from that reality.

The Cargo Claim Evidence Checklist (What Makes a Claim Winnable)

This is the checklist the driver completes at the dock in five minutes — and it's the difference between a paid claim and a form-letter denial. Every item should be gathered before the truck moves again:

The 5-minute dock evidence checklist

  • 4+ photos at delivery — wide shot of the load, close-ups of the damage, the damaged cartons in position, and one showing the trailer/unit number. Timestamped if your camera supports it.
  • POD with damage notation — write it directly on the POD before signing: "NOTED: 3 cartons crushed, shrink wrap torn." A signature alone is a clean delivery receipt.
  • Inspection report — if the consignee has one, get a copy signed. If not, write your own on the spot and have the receiver initial it.
  • Weigh-scale tickets — for shortage claims, the scale ticket is the proof the load is short versus what the BOL says.
  • Packing list with item values — you need this later to prove the value of what was damaged. The shipper's packing list is the source of truth.

The rule that makes this work: the driver does not leave the dock until the photos are taken and the POD is notated. Once the truck rolls, the evidence window is closed. That five-minute habit — not a better demand letter — is what wins claims.

The 48-Hour Damage Response Protocol

When a driver reports damage, the clock is running. Here's the sequence that preserves the claim:

Step 1 — Document at delivery (driver, at the dock)

  • Take 4+ photos. Note the damage on the POD before signing. Get an inspection report or write one and get it initialed.

Step 2 — File written notice within 48 hours (office)

  • Email the broker and the shipper a written notice of loss. Include the load number, delivery date, a description of the damage, and attach the photos. A phone call is not notice — put it in writing and keep the sent email.

Step 3 — Mitigate (don't destroy evidence)

  • Salvage what can be salvaged, but do not discard damaged freight without the broker's sign-off. Destroying evidence is a fast way to convert a winnable claim into a denied one.

Step 4 — Gather the paperwork (office)

  • Pull the BOL, rate confirmation, invoice, packing list with values, inspection report, and scale tickets into one folder. This is your evidence file.

Step 5 — File the formal claim with a demand letter (before the deadline)

  • Send the formal claim with a demand letter: the amount claimed, itemized values, the evidence, and a clear statement of what you're owed. File it well inside the 9-month window — don't wait until month eight.

The Denial-Reason Playbook

Brokers deny claims with the same seven reasons on rotation. Here's the rebuttal for each:

Denial Reason The Rebuttal
"No damage noted on POD" Photos at delivery + witness statements from the dock. A notated POD is always better, but photos can overcome a clean POD if they're timestamped.
"Improper packaging" The shipper, not the carrier, packed the freight. Point to the BOL and the shipper's loading responsibility.
"Claim filed late" Prove written notice was given within 48 hours. Forward the sent email with its timestamp.
"Concealed damage" Produce the unpacking report filed within 5 days of delivery.
"No proof of value" The invoice + packing list with itemized values.
"Pre-existing damage" Pre-trip inspection photos taken at pickup.
"Clean delivery receipt signed" Hardest to overcome. This is why the dock protocol is the whole game — prevention beats rebuttal every time.

Preventing the Next Claim: The Loading Dock Checklist

The cheapest claim is the one that never happens. Five minutes of discipline at pickup and delivery stops the majority of claims before they start:

The count-and-photo rule

  • Pre-trip inspection photos at pickup — document the freight's condition before you touch it. This is your "pre-existing damage" rebuttal later.
  • Load securement documentation — photo your straps, bars, and blocking after loading. If a claim alleges load shift, you have the securement proof.
  • Temperature logs for reefers — a reefer claim without a temperature log is nearly impossible to defend. Pull the log at delivery.
  • Count and photo at delivery — the same 5-minute dock checklist from above, every single delivery, no exceptions.

This pairs naturally with the paperwork you're already tracking. If your PODs go missing before billing, or you're not auditing your freight bills for overcharges, the same document discipline closes both gaps at once.

Frequently asked questions

How do I file a freight damage claim as a small carrier?

The process has three deadlines you cannot miss. First, the driver documents the damage at delivery: take 4+ photos, and write the damage notation directly on the POD ("NOTED: 3 cartons crushed") before signing. Second, the office files a written notice of claim to the broker or carrier within 48 hours — a phone call does not count, it must be in writing. Third, you file the formal claim with your demand letter within the Carmack Amendment's 9-month window (some brokers write shorter windows into their contracts, so check your broker-carrier agreement). Missing the 48-hour notice is the single most common reason small-carrier claims get denied.

What documentation do I need to win a cargo claim?

The evidence that determines whether a claim is winnable is almost always created at the dock, not the office. You need: 4+ photos at delivery showing the damage, a POD with an explicit damage notation (not just a signature), a delivery inspection report, the original bill of lading and rate confirmation, the invoice showing item values, and weigh-scale tickets if the load is short. For concealed damage discovered after the driver left, you need an unpacking report filed within 5 days. Claims are won or lost on this paperwork — if the POD is clean and there are no photos, the claim is almost impossible to win regardless of what actually happened.

How long do I have to file a freight claim?

There are two separate clocks. The notice of loss or damage must be given in writing within 48 hours of delivery (a contract and best-practice requirement that brokers enforce aggressively). The formal written claim itself must be filed within 9 months of delivery under the Carmack Amendment, which governs interstate carrier liability. Some broker-carrier agreements shorten these windows, so always read the claims section of your rate confirmation and broker agreement. Track both dates in the claim tracker — the deadline that matters is the shorter of the contract window and the 9-month statutory window.

Why do freight claims get denied?

The seven most common denial reasons are: "no damage noted on the POD" (rebut with photos and witness statements), "improper packaging" (rebut by showing the shipper packed the freight), "claim filed late" (rebut by proving written notice was given within 48 hours), "concealed damage not reported" (rebut with an unpacking report filed within 5 days), "no proof of value" (rebut with the invoice and packing list), "pre-existing damage" (rebut with pre-trip inspection photos), and "clean delivery receipt signed" — the hardest to overcome, which is why the dock protocol is the whole game.

Can I file a cargo claim without a lawyer or claims service?

Yes, for most claims. Self-manage anything under roughly $2,500 — the 25-50% cut a claims service takes would eat most of a small recovery anyway. The process is procedural, not legal: document at delivery, file written notice within 48 hours, assemble the evidence checklist, send a demand letter with the claim amount and itemized values, then negotiate. For claims over $5,000, a specialist's higher recovery rate can justify their cut. You do not need a lawyer to file a valid Carmack Amendment claim — you need the paperwork and the deadline discipline, both of which the free tracker in this guide handles.

How much cargo damage do small trucking companies lose per year?

Small carriers typically eat 1-3% of revenue in unrecovered cargo damage each year. For a carrier doing $1.2M in revenue, that's $12,000-$36,000 annually — and the bulk of it is recoverable if the claim is documented and filed correctly. The average is dragged up by single events: one $11,000 quarter of denied claims is not unusual for a small fleet that has no claim system. The frustrating part is that most of these claims were legitimately recoverable — they died on a procedural miss like an unsigned POD or a phone call instead of a written notice.
