Key takeaways

Your Shopify or Amazon dashboard shows revenue growing — not profit. After COGS, shipping, fulfillment, returns, and ad spend, many of your top-selling SKUs may be losing money on every unit. The Revenue Trap: When Growing Sales Means Shrinking Profits

An eight hundred SKU e-commerce brand doing two point four million dollars in annual revenue discovered that sixty percent of their revenue came from products with negative margins. They had been funding losses with volume for eighteen months — and the Shopify dashboard never told them.

This is not unusual. The dashboard reports gross sales. It subtracts discounts and refunds, sure. But it does not subtract your landed product cost. It does not subtract the shipping label you bought. It does not subtract the pick-pack fee your three PL charged. It does not subtract the cost of the unit that got returned and could not be resold. And it certainly does not subtract the Facebook ad you ran to sell that particular SKU.

What the dashboard calls "revenue" and what your bank account calls "deposits" are two completely different numbers. The gap between them is where your profit lives — or dies.

The sixty-forty trap in one sentence:

Sixty percent of your revenue could be coming from SKUs that lose money after all costs, and your dashboard will cheerfully report them as winners. The only way to know is to calculate true cost per SKU — yourself

True Cost Per SKU: The Six Numbers Your Dashbord Does Not Show You

Here are the six cost categories that separate phantom profit from real profit. Most e-commerce operators track two or three of them. Tracking all six is what turns a revenue report into a profitability report.

Cost CategoryWhat It IncludesTypical Range
1. Landed COGS Unit cost from supplier plus inbound freight plus duties plus tariffs. Not just the unit price — the fully landed cost to get the product to your warehouse. twenty to sixty percent of retail price
2. Outbound Shipping What you pay for the shipping label, not what you charge the customer. If you charge nine dollars ninety nine for shipping but the label costs twelve dollars, that two dollar one cent loss hits your margin. five to fifteen dollars per order
3. Fulfillment Fees Three PL pick-pack fees, FBA fees, warehouse receiving, and any special handling or kitting charges. These are per-unit fees that scale with volume. two to eight dollars per unit
4. Return Costs Return shipping, restocking labor, inspection, repackaging, and the write-off cost of units that cannot be resold. For apparel, this can be twenty to thirty percent of units sold. fifteen to thirty-five percent of return rate × unit cost
5. Ad Spend Attribution Amazon PPC or Meta ads allocated to specific SKUs. Not total ad spend divided by total orders — that averages your winners with your losers. Per-SKU attribution. five to twenty-five percent of unit price
6. Marketplace Fees Amazon referral fee (typically fifteen percent), Shopify transaction fee, payment processor fee, and any channel-specific fees. These are percentage-based and non-negotiable. three to seventeen percent of selling price

Here is a worked example. A product selling for fifty dollars:

  • Revenue: fifty dollars
  • Landed COGS: minus twenty two dollars
  • Outbound shipping cost: minus eight dollars
  • Fulfillment fee: minus five dollars
  • Return cost allocation: minus three dollars
  • Ad spend attribution: minus seven dollars
  • Marketplace fee (fifteen percent): minus seven dollars fifty cents
  • Net profit: negative two dollars fifty cents per unit

This product — which the dashboard calls a fifty dollar seller — actually loses two dollars fifty cents every time someone buys it. Sell ten thousand units and you have funded a twenty five thousand dollar loss with volume. And if this is your number two bestseller, you have been celebrating a disaster.

Free SKU Profitability Spreadsheet — How It Works

This Google Sheets template calculates true profit per SKU using the six cost categories above. You enter your order data once per month — exported from Shopify, Amazon, or your three PL — and the sheet shows you which products earn money and which ones burn it.

The spreadsheet has four tabs:

  • SKU Master: One row per SKU. Enter unit cost, landed COGS, shipping cost, fulfillment fee, return rate, and selling price. Columns auto-calculate gross margin, net margin, and margin percentage.
  • Monthly P-and-L: Enter monthly units sold and ad spend per SKU. The sheet calculates monthly profit and flags negative-margin SKUs in red.
  • Kill/Keep/Grow Dashboard: Every SKU is sorted into one of three buckets based on margin. One glance tells you which products to cut, which to protect, and which to double down on.
  • Ad Spend ROI: For SKUs with attributed ad spend, calculates ROAS (return on ad spend) and flags products where ads cost more than the margin they generate.

How to populate it — first time setup (approximately two to three hours)

  1. Export your orders from Shopify (Orders → Export → last thirty days) or Amazon (Reports → Fulfillment → Al Orders). Save as CSV.
  2. Export your ad spend from Amazon Ads console or Meta Ads Manager. Break out spend by ASIN or product if possible. If not, allocate by percentage of revenue per SKU — it is better than ignoring ads entirely.
  3. Get your three PL invoice for the same period. Your three PL bill already breaks out pick-pack fees and storage by SKU. Use it.
  4. Enter your landed COGS for each SKU. Include what you pay the supplier, plus freight, plus duties — even if these come from separate invoices.
  5. Calculate your actual return rate per SKU over the last ninety days. Not your site-wide average — per-SKU. Your bestseller might have a two percent return rate while that one dress has twenty-eight percent.

After the first setup, monthly refresh takes about ten minutes: drop in the new order and ad spend data, update any COGS changes, and the dashboard updates automatically.

The Kill/Keep/Grow Framework: What to Do When You See the Real Numbers

Once the spreadsheet is populated, every SKU falls into one of three buckets. Here is what to do with each:

Bucket One: Kill (Negative margin after all costs)

These SKUs lose money on every unit sold. They may be your volume darlings — your dashboard shows them as top sellers — but after COGS, shipping, fulfillment, returns, and ads, they are cash-negative. Options: raise the price (can you get away with it?), renegotiate supplier cost (can you hit a volume tier?), drop free shipping, reduce ad spend, or discontinue. Do not keep selling a product at a loss just because "it drives traffic." Traffic at a loss is not traffic — it is a subsidy you pay to customers.

Bucket Two: Keep (Margin positive but thin — zero to fifteen percent)

These products make money, but not much. They are fine as part of your catalog but should not be where you invest growth capital. Monitor these SKUs monthly — a supplier price increase of ten percent can push them from thin-profit to Kil in one cycle. If a Keep SKU also has high return rates, the combined economics may put it in Kil territory.

Bucket Three: Grow (Margin over fifteen percent, strong unit economics)

These are your real business. Every marginal ad dollar should go here. These SKUs have enough margin to absorb ad spend, absorb occasional returns, and still leave net cash in the bank. Identify the common characteristics of your Grow SKUs — category, price point, supplier, customer demographic — and use that pattern to evaluate new products before you launch them.

The hardest part:

Your bestseller by revenue may be in the Killl bucket. Deleting a product that generates hundreds of thousands in topline revenue feels wrong — but if every unit sold costs more than it brings in, keeping it is the wrong decision. Revenue is vanity. Margin is sanity. This is not a motivational poster; it is math.

Monthly Refresh: Ten Minutes to Know Your Numbers

Here is the ten-minute monthly ritual that keeps your profitability view current:

  1. Export orders for the trailing thirty days. Drop into the Monthly P-and-L tab.
  2. Update ad spend per SKU or allocate by revenue share. Ten minutes of honesty is better than zero minutes of precision.
  3. Check return rates — did any SKU's return rate spike? A jump from five percent to eighteen percent on a fifty dollar product with twenty dollar COGS means your per-unit profit dropped by six dollars fifty cents. Find out why.
  4. Review the Kill/Keep/Grow dashboard. Any SKUs that moved from Grow to Keep? Any from Keep to Kil? Act on the movers first.
  5. Spot-check three SKUs manually — the spreadsheet is only as accurate as the data you feed it. Pick one Grow, one Keep, one Kil and trace the numbers back to source documents. If the category is right, the spreadsheet is right.

This is not a ninety-minute analytics marathon. It is a ten-minute discipline repeated twelve times a year that compounds into knowing your business better than any dashboard can tell you.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate true profitability per SKU for my e-commerce store?

True SKU profitability requires subtracting six cost categories from revenue: landed COGS (product plus inbound freight plus duties), outbound shipping (what you pay, not what you charge), fulfillment fees (three PL pick-pack or FBA fees), return costs (return shipping plus restocking plus damage write-off as a percentage of units sold), ad spend attribution (Amazon PPC or Meta ads allocated per SKU), and marketplace fees. Our free spreadsheet handles all six categories and shows net margin per SKU. Most dashboards show you one or two of these; the gap is where profit disappears.

Why does my Shopify dashboard show I am profitable when my bank account does not?

Shopify reports gross sales minus discounts and refunds — it does not subtract cost of goods sold, shipping costs, fulfillment fees, marketplace fees, return costs, or advertising spend. A product selling for fifty dollars with a twenty-five dollar COGS, eight dollar shipping, five dollar fulfillment fee, and seven dollar ad spend generates five dollars in net profit — but Shopify shows the full fifty dollars in revenue. Multiply this gap across hundreds of SKUs and add return costs, and the dashboard profit can be phantom profit that never reaches your bank account. Our free spreadsheet bridges this gap.

How much does SKU profitability analysis software cost vs a spreadsheet?

SKU-level analytics SaaS platforms like Triple Whale, Polar Analytics, and Glew range from one hundred to five hundred dollars per month. Shopify apps like Lfetimey and BeProfit cost twenty to one hundred dollars per month. Our free Google Sheets template costs zero dollars and takes approximately two to three hours to populate with your order data for the first time, then ten minutes per month to update. It does not auto-sync with Shopify — you will export CSVs — but for businesses under two million in revenue testing whether they need paid analytics, free is the right first step. When you hit the point where manual CSV export is your bottleneck, you know it is time to upgrade — and you will know exactly which metrics matter.

What if my ad spend cannot be broken out by SKU?

Allocate by percentage of revenue. If SKU A generates thirty percent of monthly revenue, assign it thirty percent of total brand ad spend. This is imperfect — some SKUs convert better from ads than others — but it is far better than excluding ad spend from your profitability calculation entirely. The goal of the free spreadsheet is directionally correct numbers you can act on, not audit-grade precision. As you scale, invest in per-SKU ad attribution tooling.
