How to download and process Amazon fee reports without accounting software

This is the step most sellers skip because it looks complicated. It's not. Here's the exact 5-minute weekly process:

  1. Go to Seller Central → Reports → Payments → Date Range Report. Select "Generate Report." Choose "Summary" view. Set the date range to the past 7 days (Monday–Sunday for a clean weekly cadence).
  2. Click "Generate" and wait 30–60 seconds. Amazon builds the CSV. When it's ready, click "Download."
  3. Open the CSV, copy all rows. Paste into Tab 1 of your tracker spreadsheet starting at row 2 (row 1 is column headers — match them once, lock that row).
  4. Tab 3 auto-updates. The SUMIF formulas pull per-SKU totals from Tab 1. You don't touch Tab 3 — it just works. Watch the margin % column. Anything in red? That's your action item this week.
  5. Repeat for eBay, Walmart, Shopify. Each marketplace has its own transaction/payout report. Download them, paste into new rows in Tab 1 with a channel label. Tab 4 rolls it all up.

Total time: 30 minutes per week for 4 channels. That's 2 hours a month — versus the $15,000 in invisible fees you're currently paying per quarter without knowing which SKUs are the problem.

The fee audit: Amazon makes mistakes

Amazon processes billions of transactions. They make errors. Here are the most common fee overcharges — all of which you can catch with your tracker and file for reimbursement:

  • Returned item never actually returned. Customer files a return, Amazon refunds them, but the item was never scanned back into FBA. You paid for a return that didn't happen. Amazon reimburses these — but only if you file within 18 months. Check your returns report monthly against refunds.
  • Dimensional weight miscalculation. Amazon measures your product's dimensions and weight when it arrives at FBA. Sometimes they get it wrong — a 12 oz product measured at 2 lbs means you're paying the next size tier's FBA fee on every unit. Compare your actual dimensions to what Amazon has on file (Inventory → Manage Inventory → click the SKU → check dimensions). File a remeasurement request if it's wrong.
  • FBA fee charged at wrong size tier. Similar to dimensional weight — if your product gets classified as "Large Standard" when it should be "Small Standard," you're overpaying roughly $2–3 per unit. At 50 units/month, that's $1,200–$1,800/year.
  • Storage fee for items already removed. You created a removal order. The units shipped. Amazon's system sometimes continues charging storage for 1–2 more billing cycles. Check your monthly storage report against removal order confirmations.
  • Inbound shipment quantity discrepancies. You shipped 100 units. Amazon received 96. Four units went missing at the warehouse. After 30 days, you can file for reimbursement — but only if you have the shipment reconciliation data to prove the discrepancy. Amazon's automated reimbursement sometimes covers these; sometimes it doesn't.

The average seller recovers $500–$2,000 per year in fee overcharges. Amazon's reimbursement window is 18 months. If you've never audited your fees, you're sitting on 18 months of potential reimbursements that expire every day you don't check. The fee audit tab in the tracker flags all of the above automatically — it compares your expected fees per unit to what you were actually charged and highlights any discrepancy over 5%.

Frequently asked questions

How do I track Amazon seller fees without paying for software?

Download your Date Range Report from Seller Central (Reports → Payments → Date Range Report → Generate → Summary view), paste into a Google Sheets tracker with columns for revenue, referral fee, FBA fee, storage fee, advertising cost, and returns cost, then use SUMIF formulas to calculate per-SKU profitability. The entire weekly process takes about 30 minutes — 5 minutes to download and paste, 25 minutes to review results and take action on unprofitable SKUs. No paid software required. However, if you're selling $1M+ annually across 100+ SKUs on multiple channels, the $19–49/month for tools like A2X or GETIDA that auto-import settlement data usually pays for itself in time savings alone — the decision is about whether the 2 hours/month you spend pasting data is more expensive than the subscription.

Which Amazon fees are hidden and how do I find them?

The most commonly overlooked fees are: (1) long-term storage fees for inventory over 181 days — these don't appear on per-order reports, only on monthly storage reports; (2) returns processing fees — Amazon charges you the full FBA fee to process a return, even if the item goes back into sellable inventory; (3) disposal and removal order fees; (4) inbound placement fees for shipments that aren't split to Amazon's preferred distribution; (5) unplanned service fees for prep/labeling violations. Find them all in the Date Range Report under the "Summary" view — it breaks out every fee type as a separate column. The key insight: returns processing fees are a separate line item from the original FBA fee, and sellers often miss them because a return shows as a negative revenue line, not a separate fee line. Your CPA sees the total. You need to see the breakdown.

How do I know which SKUs are profitable on Amazon?

The full formula: Net Profit = Revenue − Referral Fee − FBA Fee − Storage Fee Allocation − PPC Allocation − Returns Cost − COGS − Disposal/Removal Costs. Most sellers only subtract referral fee and COGS, which makes every SKU look profitable. The full calculation typically reveals that 20–30% of a catalog is losing money after all fees. Flag any SKU with margin under 15% after the full calculation — that's the minimum viable margin for a product that needs to cover overhead, returns, and growth. For slow-moving inventory, the storage fee allocation is especially dangerous: a product sitting 200+ days can accumulate storage fees exceeding 30% of its retail price without you noticing because the fee appears on a completely separate monthly report from the order-level fees.

How do I reconcile Amazon settlement reports with bank deposits?

Amazon settlements never match bank deposits exactly because of: (1) the 14-day payment cycle that doesn't align with calendar months, (2) reserve holds — Amazon withholds a percentage against chargebacks, (3) prior-period adjustments appearing in current settlements, and (4) advertising costs deducted on a separate billing cycle. The reconciliation formula is: Current Period Revenue − Current Period Fees + Prior Period Adjustments − Reserve Increase = Actual Bank Deposit. Track settlement IDs to bank deposits in your spreadsheet. When your CPA asks why the numbers don't match — and they will — you'll have the answer in one row instead of spending 3 hours pulling reports from four different Seller Central pages.

What free tools can I use instead of paid Amazon accounting software?

The core stack is free: Google Sheets for the tracker, Amazon's Date Range Report (free to download), and your existing Seller Central access. For sellers doing under $500K/year, this handles 90% of what paid software does — the 10% gap is automated data import and QuickBooks/Xero posting. If you need auto-import without paying, you can use Amazon's SP-API (free for developers, but requires technical setup) to pull settlement data programmatically. For most sellers, the manual paste approach is the right trade-off: 30 minutes per week versus $19–49/month. The moment that calculus flips (saving 2 hours/month is worth $49), switch to A2X or GETIDA. But start with the spreadsheet — you'll understand your numbers better after 4 weeks of manually pasting data than you would after a year of auto-imported reports you never actually read.
