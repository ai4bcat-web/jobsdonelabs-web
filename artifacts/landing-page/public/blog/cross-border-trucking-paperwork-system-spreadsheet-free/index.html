The five documents every cross-border load needs

There are exactly five documents required for every cross-border truck load into Canada — and a sixth if you are hauling food. Miss any one of them and the truck does not clear. Here they are in plain English, not CBP-speak:

The cross-border paperwork checklist

  1. ACE e-manifest (US-bound) or ACI e-manifest (Canada-bound). This is the electronic pre-filing that tells customs what is on your truck before the truck arrives. Filed through the CBP ACE Portal or a third-party filing service. Must be submitted at least one hour before the truck reaches the border crossing. You will get a confirmation number — write it down. If the filing is rejected, you will not know until the truck is at the booth unless you check.
  2. PAPS sticker number (US-bound) or PARS sticker number (Canada-bound). PAPS stands for Pre-Arrival Processing System — it is a barcode sticker with a unique shipment control number that links the physical paperwork in the driver's hand to the electronic ACE manifest in the system. The sticker goes on the invoice and the bill of lading. When the CBP officer scans it at the booth, they see your pre-filed manifest. If the PAPS number on the invoice does not match the number in the ACE filing, the officer sends you to secondary. PARS is the identical system on the Canada-bound side. Your broker usually provides these stickers — but you must verify the number matches your ACE filing before the driver leaves.
  3. Customs invoice. Not your standard freight invoice — a customs invoice includes harmonized system tariff codes, country of origin for every line item, and the value for duty. Both CBP and CBSA use this to assess duties and taxes. Your shipper or freight forwarder should provide this, but many do not know how to fill one out correctly. The carrier is ultimately responsible for presenting accurate customs documentation at the border.
  4. Customs broker power of attorney (POA). Your customs broker cannot file entry on your behalf without a valid POA on file. This is a one-time setup per broker, but POAs expire — usually annually — and when they expire, your broker's filings get rejected without warning. You need a current POA on file before the first load crosses, and you need to track expiry dates.
  5. Customs bond. A continuous customs bond is required to move goods across the US border commercially. The bond guarantees that CBP will collect duties, taxes, and fees even if the importer does not pay. For Canada-bound loads, you need a Temporary Importation Bond or your customer's broker must handle it. Bonds have minimum coverage amounts based on your annual import volume, and like the POA, they expire.

If you are hauling food products — produce, meat, dairy, seafood, pet food, or anything regulated by the FDA — there is a sixth document: FDA Prior Notice. This must be filed electronically with the FDA before the food arrives at the border. The timeline is tighter than ACE: two hours before arrival for road shipments, four hours for rail or air, and eight hours for water. If Prior Notice is not filed or is filed incorrectly, the FDA can refuse the shipment — the truck does not enter, and the carrier eats the return freight cost.

Canada versus Mexico: why the paperwork is not the same at both borders

Small carriers who cross into Canada sometimes assume crossing into Mexico works the same way. It does not. Canada uses PARS and ACI eManifest — straightforward electronic pre-filing with barcode stickers. Mexico requires the Carta Porte, a supplemental document to the electronic invoice that describes the cargo, the vehicle, the route, and the driver. Mexican customs also requires a FIRMS code — Facility Information and Resources Management System code — for the US-side port of exit. And if the load is a through-shipment that enters Mexico and continues to Central America, the paperwork multiplies again.

For most small US carriers, the Canada lane is the practical starting point. The paperwork system in the free spreadsheet covers both borders, but the Canada tab is the one that will get used first. The Mexico tab is there when you need it, with Carta Porte requirements, FIRMS code lookup, and the list of Mexican customs brokers who handle truck freight at each major crossing.

The free cross-border paperwork spreadsheet — a walkthrough

The spreadsheet has six tabs. Each one solves a specific failure mode that small fleets hit in their first six months of cross-border hauling. Here is how each tab works:

Tab 1: Per-Load Border Checklist

This is the only tab your dispatcher needs to open before a cross-border load departs. It is a seven-item checklist printed or viewed per load:

  • ACE or ACI filed? (Y/N — with confirmation number field)
  • PAPS or PARS sticker on invoice? (Y/N — with sticker number field)
  • Customs invoice complete with HS codes? (Y/N)
  • Broker POA active? (auto-populates from Tab 4 — green if current, red if expired)
  • Bond active? (auto-populates from Tab 5 — green if current, red if expired)
  • FDA Prior Notice filed? (Y/N — for food loads only, grayed out otherwise)
  • Driver has printed Border Instruction Card? (Y/N — card prints from Tab 6)

If all seven items are green, the load is cleared to depart. If any field is red, the truck does not move until the issue is resolved. This takes the "I thought the broker handled that" out of the equation — either the checklist is green or the truck waits.

Tab 2: ACE and ACI Filing Tracker

Log every cross-border filing with: load reference number, border crossing, ACE or ACI filing date and time, filed-by (in-house or third-party service), confirmation number, and a "verified accepted" checkbox. The tracker calculates hours-until-border-crossing and flags any filing where the window is under ninety minutes — because if your driver hits a two-hour border wait and the manifest was filed exactly sixty minutes before the scheduled crossing, CBP sees an expired filing and holds the truck.

Tab 3: PAPS and PARS Sticker Log

Each PAPS or PARS sticker has a unique control number. This tab logs: sticker number, ACE or ACI manifest number it is linked to, load reference, date issued, and whether it has been used (preventing the disaster of reusing a PAPS number — which guarantees a secondary inspection because the old manifest is still in the system). The sticker log also tracks which broker provided the stickers, because if a broker runs out of pre-printed stickers mid-week, you need to know immediately.

Tab 4: Customs Broker Contact Sheet

If you run multiple lanes, you likely have different brokers for different border crossings. This tab stores: broker name, contact person, phone number, after-hours emergency number, email, border crossings they handle, POA expiry date, and a notes field for "call Brian at this broker, not Sarah — Sarah changed firms in June." When the POA expiry date is within thirty days, the field turns yellow as a warning. When expired, it turns red.

Tab 5: Bond Expiry Calendar

Continuous customs bonds typically renew annually. This tab tracks: bond type (US Customs, Temporary Importation), bond number, coverage amount, issuing surety company, effective date, expiry date, and renewal contact. Same thirty-day yellow warning and red expired flag. The bond expiry tracker also calculates your annual import volume (from the filing tracker log) against your bond coverage amount — because if your volume grows past your bond limit, CBP can issue a penalty for insufficient bond coverage.

Tab 6: FDA Prior Notice Tracker

For food loads only. Tracks: FDA prior notice confirmation number, product description, FDA product code, manufacturer or shipper registration number, country of production, and estimated arrival time at the border. The tracker calculates the two-hour, four-hour, and eight-hour filing windows based on transport mode and flags late filings automatically. When no food loads are in transit, this tab stays blank — it is there for the week you pick up a frozen vegetable load and realize two hours before the border that nobody mentioned Prior Notice.

The four-hour pre-border routine that prevents ninety percent of border holds

When your driver is four hours from the border crossing, someone — the dispatcher, a night-shift coordinator, or the driver at a rest stop — runs a four-step verification. This takes ten minutes and catches the issues that cause almost every border hold:

  1. Verify ACE or ACI accepted. Log into the CBP ACE Portal or your third-party filing service dashboard. Confirm the manifest status is "Accepted" — not "Submitted," not "Pending." Write down the confirmation number. If it was rejected, you have three hours to fix and resubmit. If you do not check until the booth, you have zero minutes.
  2. Confirm PAPS or PARS sticker on invoice. The driver should physically look at the invoice and read the sticker number back. Then cross-reference it against the ACE or ACI manifest confirmation. They must match. If the sticker fell off (it happens), the broker can email a replacement number — but only if you catch it before the truck reaches the booth.
  3. Text or call the customs broker. A thirty-second message: "Load reference X, crossing at Y crossing, ACE confirmation number Z, any issues?" If the broker sees a problem — missing HS code, expired POA, bond issue — you have time to fix it. If you do not ask until the truck is already at secondary inspection, the fix takes hours instead of minutes.
  4. Check border wait times. CBP publishes real-time border wait times for commercial vehicles at every major crossing on their website and mobile app. If the wait is over two hours at your planned crossing, the one-hour ACE filing window may close before the truck even reaches the booth. Consider an alternate crossing if the delay is extreme — the extra driving time may be less than the detention cost of waiting at a backed-up crossing with an expired manifest.

The Driver Border Instruction Card — the one-pager that saves you from the most common failure mode

Here is what actually happens at the booth: the CBP officer asks the driver for the PAPS number, the ACE confirmation, and the broker's contact. The driver — who was hired to drive a truck, not to be a customs specialist — opens their phone, scrolls through texts from three different people, finds a number that might be the PAPS but might be the load confirmation number, and hands the phone to the officer. The officer sees a number that does not match anything in the system and sends the truck to secondary.

The fix is so simple it is almost embarrassing: a printed card, the size of a business card, that the driver keeps clipped to the visor. The card has exactly five lines:

BORDER CARD — LOAD REF: _______
ACE: _______   ·   PAPS: _______
BROKER: _______   ·   PH: _______
BOND #: _______
CROSSING: _______   ·   FILED: _______
IF HELD: CALL BROKER FIRST, THEN DISPATCH

The dispatcher fills it out before the load departs, hands it to the driver, and the driver clips it to the visor. When the officer asks, the driver hands over the card along with the invoice and bill of lading. The officer sees clean, matching numbers, scans the PAPS barcode, and waves the truck through. Three minutes at the booth instead of six hours in secondary.

This card costs zero dollars and prevents the single most common failure mode in cross-border trucking: the driver at the booth not knowing the right number to give the officer.

What to do when it goes wrong anyway — the border jail escalation script

Even with a perfect system, things go wrong. A filing service has an outage. A broker's POA expired at midnight and nobody caught it. A CBP officer flags the load for a random inspection. When your truck is sitting at secondary inspection, you need an escalation order — who to call, in what sequence, with what information — not a panicked group text to four people who each assume someone else is handling it.

The escalation sequence

  1. Driver calls customs broker — immediately. Most holds are documentation issues the broker can fix remotely. The driver needs to tell the broker: the PAPS number, the ACE confirmation number, the crossing location, and the lane number or booth number where they are parked. The broker pulls up the filing, identifies the issue, and either fixes it on the spot or tells the driver what additional information is needed.
  2. If the broker cannot fix it within one hour: Dispatcher calls the broker's after-hours emergency line, not the driver. The driver's job is to stay with the truck. The dispatcher gets a status update and escalates internally if needed — but most issues are resolved at this level.
  3. If CBP has placed a customs hold unrelated to documentation: There is no shortcut. The broker cannot override a CBP inspection hold. The dispatcher notifies the customer with: what is happening, where the truck is, what CBP has communicated (if anything), and a revised ETA based on the average secondary inspection time at that crossing. The customer will be frustrated — but they will be far less frustrated than if they find out four hours later with no update.
  4. After release: Document what happened — what the issue was, how it was resolved, and what system change prevents it from happening again. Every border hold is a process gap. If it happened once, it will happen again unless the checklist or the pre-border routine changes.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a customs broker for every cross-border load?

Yes. US and Canadian customs regulations require a licensed customs broker to file entry on behalf of the importer for commercial shipments. The carrier does not hire the broker — the importer or shipper does — but the carrier must verify that a broker is assigned and a valid POA is on file before the load crosses. If you arrive at the border and the importer has not assigned a broker, the load cannot clear. Some third-party ACE filing services bundle broker services with manifest filing, which is the fastest setup route for new cross-border carriers.

How much does a continuous customs bond cost for a small carrier?

A continuous customs bond for a small carrier doing a few hundred cross-border loads per year typically costs between two hundred and five hundred dollars annually, depending on the bond amount which is set at ten percent of your estimated annual duties, taxes, and fees with a minimum of fifty thousand dollars in coverage. Single-entry bonds are available for occasional cross-border loads — typically two to five dollars per thousand dollars of bond coverage — but if you cross more than twice per year, a continuous bond is cheaper.

Can I file ACE e-manifest myself or do I need a third-party service?

You can file ACE e-manifest directly through the CBP ACE Secure Data Portal — it is free to register and file. However, the CBP portal has a steep learning curve, requires a SCAC code (Standard Carrier Alpha Code) from the National Motor Freight Traffic Association, and the interface is not built for speed. Most small carriers use a third-party filing service that charges per-load fees — typically five to fifteen dollars — for a much faster interface. Some services integrate directly with popular TMS platforms and load boards, pulling shipment data automatically so the dispatcher does not re-key information. If you are doing more than five cross-border loads per week, the time savings of a third-party service usually outweigh the per-load cost.

What is the difference between a PAPS sticker and a PARS sticker?

Functionally, they are the same thing for different borders: PAPS (Pre-Arrival Processing System) is used for shipments entering the United States, and PARS (Pre-Arrival Review System) is used for shipments entering Canada. Both are barcode stickers with a unique control number that links the physical paperwork to the electronic manifest. The systems themselves are separate — a PAPS number filed with CBP will not work with CBSA, and a PARS number filed with CBSA will not work with CBP. Your broker or filing service will provide the correct sticker type for the direction you are crossing. A single cross-border round trip requires both: a PARS sticker to enter Canada on the northbound leg and a PAPS sticker to re-enter the US on the southbound leg.
