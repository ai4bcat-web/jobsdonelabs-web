Your #2 Bestseller Was Out of Stock for 11 Days and Nobody Noticed. Here's the Stockout Prevention Tracker That Fixes It.

This free reorder point spreadsheet uses lead time, daily sales velocity, and safety stock to tell you what to rebuy before the shelf is empty. What a stockout actually costs

The direct math is brutal: a SKU doing $4,200/month that's out of stock for 11 days loses $1,540 in sales you'll never recover. But the real damage runs deeper.

On Amazon, a stockout tanks your Best Sellers Rank and keyword position — and the ranking penalty persists for weeks after you restock because the algorithm treats your listing as unreliable. On Shopify, repeat customers who hit an "out of stock" page open a competitor's tab and often never come back. Across both channels, stockouts tell the marketplace that your product isn't reliable — and algorithms penalize unreliability.

The hidden multiplier: A customer who buys from you every 6 weeks and hits a stockout on week 6 doesn't just skip one order. They find a substitute, and if that substitute arrives on time and works fine, you've lost permanent repeat revenue — not just $1,540 from one incident. The lifetime value of that customer evaporates because of a spreadsheet you didn't have.

For a small retailer with 50-100 SKUs, stockouts typically drain 5-10% of potential annual revenue — quietly, invisibly, because nobody's tracking what would have sold if the shelf weren't empty.

The free stockout prevention spreadsheet

You don't need inventory software to stop stockouts. You need a spreadsheet that answers one question every Monday morning: which SKUs need a purchase order right now?

Here's what the spreadsheet tracks, per SKU:

  • Product name and SKU code — link to your catalog
  • Supplier — with contact info and typical minimum order quantity
  • Lead time (actual, not quoted) — more on this below, but track real days-to-delivery, not the supplier's brochure number
  • Average daily sales — calculated from your last 90 days of order data
  • Current stock on hand — updated weekly (or daily for A-items)
  • Reorder point — formula: (avg daily sales × lead time) + safety stock
  • Days of stock remaining — current stock ÷ avg daily sales
  • Action status — green (OK), yellow (REORDER NOW — at or below reorder point), red (CRITICAL — less than 3 days of stock remaining)

Safety stock calculator: For a new product with uncertain demand, set safety stock at 50% of lead-time demand. Example: 3 units/day × 7-day lead time = 21 units of lead-time demand. Safety stock = 10 units. Reorder point = 21 + 10 = 31 units. After 3-4 reorder cycles, adjust safety stock based on actual sales variance — if demand is steady, drop to 25%; if it swings by 40% month-to-month, keep it at 50% or higher.

Conditional formatting is the engine. Set the "Days of stock" column to highlight yellow at ≤7 days and red at ≤3 days. When you open the sheet Monday morning, your eyes go straight to the red cells — those are the SKUs that need a purchase order today. No scanning 50 rows; the color does the triage.

ABC analysis: protect your A-items first

Not all stockouts are equal. Running out of your #2 bestseller costs $1,540 in 11 days. Running out of SKU #47 — which sells 2 units a month — is a rounding error. The spreadsheet needs to know the difference.

ABC analysis ranks every SKU by revenue contribution:

  • A-items (top 20% of SKUs, ~80% of revenue): Never stock out. Review inventory weekly, build extra safety stock. These are your business — protect them first.
  • B-items (middle 30%, ~15% of revenue): Monitor bi-weekly. Stockouts are painful but recoverable. Standard safety stock is fine.
  • C-items (bottom 50%, ~5% of revenue): Stockout acceptable. Reorder when convenient, not urgent. Don't tie up cash in safety stock for SKUs that barely move.

How to run ABC analysis in 15 minutes

  1. Export your last 90 days of sales by SKU (your e-commerce platform can do this)
  2. Sort by total revenue, highest to lowest
  3. Add a "cumulative revenue %" column — each row's revenue as a percentage of total
  4. Assign A to SKUs that make up the first 80% of cumulative revenue, B to the next 15%, C to the remainder
  5. Recalculate quarterly — product mixes shift, and last quarter's B-item might be this quarter's A-item

Once classified, add the ABC tier to your reorder point spreadsheet. Now Monday morning's review tells you not just what to reorder, but what matters most.

The 5-minute weekly review

The spreadsheet is only as good as the review cadence. Here's the routine that makes it work:

  1. Monday morning, 5 minutes. Open the sheet. Sort by "days of stock remaining," ascending. The red and yellow rows are at the top.
  2. For every yellow or red A-item: Place a purchase order immediately. Don't wait for the supplier's email — initiate.
  3. For B-items in yellow: Place a PO this week. For B-items in red: PO today.
  4. For C-items: Note them. Place POs when you have a batch order that meets the supplier's minimum. A stockout on a C-item is acceptable — don't pay rush shipping for an SKU that generates $50/month.

Fifty SKUs, five minutes. The key is doing it every week — not "when you remember." Put it on your calendar. Monday at 9 AM. The spreadsheet does the math; your job is to act on the colors.

Lead time is the hidden killer

Most stockout prevention advice says "use the supplier's quoted lead time." But a supplier who quotes 7 days and consistently delivers in 10-14 will stock you out every single time — because your reorder point was calculated around 7 days, and 10 days of demand already cleared your safety stock by the time goods arrive.

Track actual lead times, not quoted. In your spreadsheet, add two columns: "PO date" and "goods received date." After 3-4 orders from the same supplier, calculate the real average. You'll typically find it's 30-50% longer than the quote. Adjust your reorder point to use the actual number.

Real example: A Shopify seller's main supplier quoted "5-7 business days." Actual tracking over 6 orders: 9, 11, 8, 10, 13, and 9 days. Average: 10 days. The seller was planning around 7 days — meaning every reorder was 3 days late. Those 3 days burned through the safety stock, causing 4 stockouts in 6 months. Fixing the lead-time number in the spreadsheet eliminated stockouts without changing anything else.

Update lead times quarterly. Suppliers change — shipping lanes shift, production schedules fill up, and the quote from 18 months ago is no longer accurate. The spreadsheet stays current when the numbers stay current.

When to graduate from spreadsheets to inventory software

The free reorder point spreadsheet handles 50-100 SKUs effortlessly. But it's not forever. Here are the three signals that say it's time to upgrade:

  1. 100+ active SKUs across multiple channels. When the Monday review takes more than an hour because you're cross-referencing Shopify, Amazon, and Etsy inventory levels, the spreadsheet's manual data entry is costing more than a $50-200/month tool would.
  2. Multi-warehouse or 3PL inventory. The spreadsheet works for one location. The moment you're splitting inventory across warehouses, FBA, and a 3PL — and need real-time sync — you need software.
  3. Stockouts or over-ordering cost you $1,000+/month. If the spreadsheet is catching 90% of problems but the 10% it misses still bleeds four figures a month, the software pays for itself in prevented losses alone.

Until you hit one of these thresholds: the spreadsheet is free, it works, and it's better than what 80% of small retailers are doing right now (which is nothing — guessing when to reorder based on memory and shelf glances).

Frequently asked questions

How is a reorder point spreadsheet different from my e-commerce platform's low-stock alerts?

Platform alerts are reactive — they tell you when stock is already low and you might be too late. A reorder point spreadsheet is proactive: it calculates how many days of stock remain based on your actual sales velocity and tells you to reorder before the low-stock alert triggers. The difference is the lead-time buffer. If your supplier takes 10 days and your platform alerts at 5 units, you're already 5 days into a stockout by the time goods arrive. The spreadsheet catches it at 10+ days of buffer.

What if my sales are seasonal? Won't the reorder point be wrong half the year?

Use a rolling 90-day average for daily sales velocity, recalculated monthly. During Q4 holiday spikes, the 90-day window naturally captures the ramp-up. During January lulls, it captures the slowdown. If your business has extreme seasonality (e.g., 70% of revenue in 8 weeks), create a separate "peak season" version of the spreadsheet with higher safety stock and shorter review intervals — switch to it 2 weeks before the season starts.

Do I need separate spreadsheets for FBA inventory vs. my own warehouse?

Start with separate tabs in the same spreadsheet — one for FBA, one for your warehouse or 3PL. The formulas are identical; the inventory counts and lead times differ. FBA lead times include the time it takes Amazon to check in and distribute your inventory across fulfillment centers, which adds 3-7 days beyond supplier shipping time. Factor that into your FBA-tab lead time.

Can I use this for a wholesale or B2B business?

Yes, but with one adjustment: wholesale orders tend to be lumpy — one $5,000 PO from a retailer rather than 50 individual consumer orders. Replace "average daily sales" with "average weekly sales" and set your review cadence to weekly. For businesses where a single large PO can deplete inventory instantly, add a "max single-order quantity" column and set a trigger: if any pending order is >50% of current stock, flag it for immediate review regardless of the reorder point calculation.
