A ten-truck fleet spends two to four hours every morning rebuilding its dispatch plan on a whiteboard, and by 10 AM two loads have canceled and a driver has called in sick. This free trucking dispatch spreadsheet builds the whole plan in twenty minutes and re-sorts it in five every time something changes.

I run a small fleet, so I've stood in front of that whiteboard more mornings than I care to count. Truck numbers, driver names, load IDs, pickup windows, all crossed out and rewritten until the thing reads like a ransom note. One driver's on a reset. Another has a doctor's appointment at 2 PM. The only truck close to the Chicago pickup just texted "truck won't start."

Here's what that morning actually costs. A ten-truck fleet moving three hundred loads a month burns two to four hours a day on dispatch. At thirty dollars an hour across two hundred fifty working days, that's roughly twenty-two thousand five hundred dollars a year. And the plan it produces is already wrong by 10 AM.

Then it breaks. Two loads cancel. One driver calls in sick. A broker calls with a "market adjustment" that shaves three hundred off a rate. Every one of those events means erasing half the board and starting over, because a whiteboard has no memory and no math. The silent killer is the uncovered load: every day a truck sits empty because a dispatch mistake left it with no reload costs between one hundred fifty and five hundred dollars. Across a year that's forty thousand dollars on the low end.

The real fix isn't a TMS. Most small fleets can't justify a five-hundred-dollar-a-month transportation management system when they're running eight trucks, and honestly the TMS is usually more software than the problem calls for. What a dispatcher actually needs is a free trucking dispatch spreadsheet with six linked tabs: a load board, a driver assignment matrix, a deadhead calculator, a rate-per-mile and profit-margin calculator, a detention clock, and a daily fleet P&L snapshot.

I wrote up the full six-tab build in this [free trucking dispatch spreadsheet](https://www.jobsdonelabs.ai/blog/trucking-dispatch-spreadsheet-free-small-fleet/) guide. Here's the short version.

The load board is one row per load, pasted in straight from the broker's rate confirmation. The driver assignment matrix is where the plan forms. Every driver is a row, every open load is a column, and each cell scores the pair on location, hours of service, equipment, and home-time preference. The best assignment surfaces on its own instead of being guessed off a smeared board.

Then the math tabs do what the whiteboard never could. The deadhead calculator shows you that a twenty-four hundred dollar load with three hundred miles of deadhead is often worse than a twenty-one hundred dollar load with fifty miles of empty. The rate-per-mile calculator catches the load that looks profitable on total revenue but loses money after empty miles and your true cost per mile. The detention clock makes sure every hour your driver sits at a dock gets billed instead of forgotten.

The last tab matters most to me: the daily P&L snapshot. One line per truck, one total for the fleet, so you can see that Truck 287 delivered this morning and then sat the rest of the day with no reload lined up. That negative one-eighty in driver pay for zero revenue is the uncovered-load leak in real time. The whiteboard hides it until month-end. The spreadsheet shows it the same day.

When a load cancels at 9:47 AM, the whole thing re-sorts in about five minutes. Mark it canceled, free the truck, re-run the assignment for that one truck, and log the broker so repeat offenders stop costing you a repositioning every month. That last step is where the leverage lives. A carrier who takes a three-hundred-dollar market adjustment three times from the same broker needs data before they can push back or blacklist them.
