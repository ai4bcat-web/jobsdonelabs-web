# Your Proposals Are Cold and It's Costing You $20K to $40K a Year — Here's the Pipeline System That Fixes It

Most solo consultants send 10+ proposals a month and follow up on three. A simple proposal pipeline tracking spreadsheet with five stages, one next-action column, and a 10-second daily sort recovers $20K to $40K in deals that were already sold. No CRM required.

I used to think pipeline tracking was overkill for a solo consultant. I was wrong.

Here's how I figured it out. I sat down one Sunday and pulled up every proposal I'd sent over the previous six months. Not the ones that closed. The ones that had just... evaporated. Sent the proposal, had a good call, prospect said they'd review it. Then nothing. Two more weeks of client work, a dozen fires, and by the time I remembered to follow up, the window had closed.

I counted 12 proposals, $4,200 average. Six of them died because I never made the third touch. Six of twelve. At a 20% recovery rate (conservative, since these people had already asked for the proposal) that's roughly $10,000 in revenue I left sitting in my own sent folder. In six months. As a solo operator, $20K a year you left on the table isn't a rounding error. It's a second kid's college fund you forgot to collect.

## The problem isn't discipline. It's visibility

Here's the thing. I didn't skip those follow-ups because I was lazy. I skipped them because proposals live in your email, and email is a river. You can't stand in a river and expect to find the same rock twice. A proposal from Tuesday disappears by Friday under client threads, vendor emails, and LinkedIn notifications.

The fix isn't willpower. The fix is a system that tells you, every single morning, who to call today. And it doesn't need to be a CRM.

## The five-stage pipeline tracker

Build this in a free spreadsheet. One tab, one row per active deal:

1. **Lead:** interest expressed, not qualified
2. **Qualified:** real need, real budget, real timeline
3. **Proposal Sent:** the clock starts here
4. **Negotiation:** scope, price, timeline are being discussed
5. **Closed (Won or Lost):** the only two exits

The columns that make it work: prospect, contact info, proposal date, proposal value, stage (dropdown), probability, **next action**, and **next action date**.

That next action date column is the whole system. Every morning, sort by it, ascending. Overdue follow-ups float to the top in red. Today's calls sit right under them. Ten seconds. That's your sales day. The top three rows.

One rule makes it self-enforcing: never end the day with a red row. Either you made the call, or you set a new date. But the overdue follow-up doesn't survive the day untouched.

## The follow-up sequence that actually closes deals

Every proposal gets five touches, not one prayer:

- **Day 1:** Send the proposal. In the cover email: "I'll follow up Thursday to walk through any questions." Never send a proposal without announcing the follow-up.
- **Day 3:** Value-add email. Not "just checking in." A case study, a relevant article, an answer to something they asked in discovery.
- **Day 7:** The call. Walk through the proposal, answer the objection they didn't put in the email, ask: "What would need to be true for us to start on the 1st?" This is where most saved deals actually close.
- **Day 14:** Final email. Short, honest. "I'll close this proposal window Friday so we can both plan our month."
- **Day 21:** Move to Closed-Lost. Drop the prospect into a 90-day nurture list. The pipeline stays honest.

Most solo professionals stop at Day 1. A proposal isn't a lottery ticket. It's a clock that starts running the moment you hit send. The spreadsheet simply makes the clock visible.

## I learned this from freight

In my brokerage, if a load goes uncovered, you see it. The truck sits empty. The customer calls. The loss has a name and a dollar figure and a timestamp. A forgotten proposal in consulting is the same math, but there's no dashboard light. Nobody calls to tell you they didn't hire you. The deal just quietly bleeds out in your outbox.

The spreadsheet is the dashboard light.

## When to graduate from the spreadsheet

Stay with the free sheet until one of three things happens: you pass 20 active deals, your team hits three people, or you're spending more time copying email threads into the notes column than actually calling prospects. At that point, buy a lightweight CRM. Before that, the spreadsheet is actually better. It has exactly the columns above and nothing else. Most CRMs are built for sales teams, not for a consultant who wants to know who to call today.

## The monthly review that catches the real leak

Last business day of the month, answer five questions in 15 minutes: how many proposals sent, what's your win rate, average time-to-close, average deal size, and stage conversion rate (what fraction of Negotiation deals actually close). The trend matters more than any single month. If win rate is flat but time-to-close is creeping up, your proposals are stalling in Negotiation and you need a close date in every conversation.
